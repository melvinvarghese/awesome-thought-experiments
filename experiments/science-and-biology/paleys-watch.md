# Paley’s Watch

**Category:** science-and-biology  
**Type:** analogy  
**Associated thinker(s):** William Paley  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Paley, Natural Theology (1802)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** design, adaptation, explanation  
**Related experiments:** [Darwin’s Imagined Gradations of the Eye](darwins-eye.md); [The Spandrels of San Marco](spandrels-of-san-marco.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Paley asks us to imagine crossing a heath and striking our foot against a stone. We might think the stone has always been there. Now suppose we find a watch. Its parts are shaped and fitted together to move in a way that shows the time. We would conclude that the watch had a maker who designed it for that purpose.

Paley argues that the eye, and other organs of living things, show an even greater degree of fitted complexity. By the same reasoning, they too must have a designer.

## 2. The Central Question

Does the appearance of design in living things support the conclusion that they were designed?

## 3. Why It Matters

The analogy is the classic statement of the argument from design. It identifies a real feature of life, adaptation, and offers an explanation. It also states in a clear form the inference that Darwin’s theory later challenged.

It remains useful for distinguishing between an argument by analogy and an inference to the best explanation.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Defenders of design** treat adaptation as evidence of purposeful intelligence. **Hume’s earlier criticisms** say the analogy between organisms and artefacts is weak. **Darwinian evolution** offers a natural process that produces adaptation without foresight. **Contemporary design arguments** shift to other features, such as the fine-tuning of the physical constants.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Paley’s argument is not a claim that evolution is false; he wrote before Darwin. Nor is the watch an example of the argument’s weakness: a watch is a human artefact, which is why the inference works in that case. The question is whether living things are relevantly similar.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What features of the watch support the inference to a maker?
2. Does the inference work if the watch could reproduce?
3. What explains the appearance of design if there is no designer?
4. Is the analogy stronger or weaker than an argument from the best explanation?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Paley considers the case of a watch that makes other watches, and argues that this would increase the case for a maker. Critics read that as an early hint of the problem he did not solve.
