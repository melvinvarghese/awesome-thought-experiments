# Paley’s Watch

**Category:** science-and-biology  
**Type:** analogy  
**Associated thinker(s):** William Paley  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Paley, *Natural Theology* (1802)\
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** design, adaptation, explanation  
**Related experiments:** [Darwin’s Imagined Gradations of the Eye](darwins-eye.md); [The Spandrels of San Marco](spandrels-of-san-marco.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Paley asks us to imagine crossing a heath and striking a stone, then finding a watch. Its parts are fitted together so that they move in a regulated way and tell the time. Paley argues that this apparent arrangement supports an inference to a maker who designed the watch for its purpose.

He applies the reasoning to the eye and other organized parts of living things, arguing that their fitted structures support the conclusion of an intelligent Creator.

## 2. The Central Question

Does the appearance of design in living things support the conclusion that they were designed?

## 3. Why It Matters

Paley’s watch is a classic formulation of a design inference. He treats the apparent fit of an organism’s parts to their functions as evidence of design and offers an intelligent Creator as its explanation. The disputed step is whether reasoning he applies to an artifact also supports his conclusion about living things.

The example can prompt comparison among analogical, design-inference, and inference-to-the-best-explanation accounts. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that Paley is often classified as making an analogical argument, but interprets his own reasoning as an inference from design-like features rather than a straightforward comparison of artifact and organism.

## 4. Major Interpretations

In Hume’s *Dialogues* (1779), Philo challenges Cleanthes’ comparison of the world with human machines, arguing that living things may offer a closer analogy. This is an earlier critique of design reasoning, not a direct reply to Paley’s 1802 book.

Darwin’s 1859 theory of natural selection supplied a natural process for explaining how functional adaptations could accumulate. It challenged Paley’s account of adaptation as evidence for design, but historical responses varied; evolution and belief in design were not treated as mutually exclusive by every contemporary. Some current design arguments focus on cosmic fine-tuning, while others address biological design or other features.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Paley’s *Natural Theology* appeared in 1802, before Darwin’s *Origin of Species* (1859); it was not an argument against evolution. The watch is a human artifact whose fitted parts Paley treats as evidence of a maker. The disputed step is whether comparable features in living things warrant the same conclusion.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What features of the watch support the inference to a maker?
2. Does the inference work if the watch could reproduce?
3. What explains the appearance of design if there is no designer?
4. Is the analogy stronger or weaker than an inference to the best explanation?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Paley imagines a watch that produces another watch and argues that reproduction does not explain the arrangement of its parts: the mechanism itself, he says, still points to design. He later applies this reasoning to plant and animal generation. A claim about how later critics interpret this passage would need a named source.
