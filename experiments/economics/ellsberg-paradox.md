# The Ellsberg Paradox

**Category:** economics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Daniel Ellsberg  
**Original publication or approximate period:** “Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms” (1961)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Themes:** ambiguity; subjective probability; sure-thing principle  
**Related experiments:** allais-paradox; two-envelopes

## 1. The Scenario

An urn contains 90 balls: 30 red and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. In the first choice, a winning bet pays the same prize if the ball is red or, alternatively, if it is black. Many favor red, whose probability is known. In the second choice, compare winning on red-or-yellow with winning on black-or-yellow. Many favor black-or-yellow, whose probability is known to be 2/3.

For a precise probability distribution, preferring red to black implies P(red) > P(black). Adding the same P(yellow) to both sides implies preference for red-or-yellow. The paired preference pattern reverses that implication.

## 2. The Central Question

Can aversion to unknown probabilities be rational even when it violates a standard subjective expected-utility representation?

## 3. Why It Matters

The urn distinguishes risk with specified probabilities from ambiguity about probabilities. Treating all uncertainty as one precise probability distribution can hide attitudes toward the reliability or completeness of information. The challenge is to explain those attitudes without abandoning coherent comparison of options.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The orthodox response** defends the sure-thing principle and asks the chooser to revise the paired preferences. **Ambiguity-sensitive approaches** use sets of probabilities or alternative evaluation rules that can represent cautious attitudes toward poorly known distributions. **Richer outcome descriptions** consider whether uncertainty itself matters to the agent. These are different theories of rationality, not evidence that the urn has inconsistent physical probabilities.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Unknown does not mean equally split. Saying that the red bet feels safer is not yet a proof of rational superiority. The paradox concerns the combination of choices: either choice alone can fit a precise probability distribution. It also differs from ordinary risk aversion over monetary gains.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would paying to inspect the mixture change the stakes of your reasoning?
2. Can you state a single probability for black that supports both of the familiar preferences?
3. Should confidence in a probability estimate influence choice independently of the estimate itself?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Two-urn versions contrast a known mixture with an unknown one. Allais’s example uses fully known probabilities and therefore tests a related axiom without the same ambiguity feature.
