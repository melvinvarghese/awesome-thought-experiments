# Pascal’s Wager

**Category:** economics  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Blaise Pascal  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Pascal, Pensées (published posthumously, 1670)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** decision under uncertainty, expected value, belief  
**Related experiments:** [The St. Petersburg Paradox](../mathematics/st-petersburg-paradox.md); [Pascal’s Mugging](pascals-mugging.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Pascal imagines that you must decide whether to live as if God exists, though reason cannot settle the matter. If you wager that God exists and you are right, you gain an infinite reward. If you are wrong, you lose something small: some pleasures and effort. If you wager against God, you gain little if you are right and risk an infinite loss if you are wrong.

Pascal argues that, even if the chance that God exists is small, the infinite size of the possible reward makes believing the rational bet.

## 2. The Central Question

Can a calculation of expected benefit make it rational to commit to a belief or way of life?

## 3. Why It Matters

The wager is among the earliest uses of decision theory in philosophy. It shows how an infinite payoff can dominate a calculation however small its probability. It also raises questions about whether belief can be chosen, whether prudential reasons can support belief, and how to treat infinite values.

Its structure reappears in modern debates about risks with tiny probabilities and enormous stakes.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The many-gods objection** notes that there are many possible gods or rewards, so the calculation does not favour one choice. **The infinite-utility objection** says that expected utility is not well-defined when infinities are involved. **The voluntarism objection** says one cannot believe at will. **Defenders** reply that one can take steps that lead to belief, and that Pascal’s version avoids some objections. **Some readers** treat it as an argument about the cost of inquiry rather than about proof.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The wager is not a proof that God exists, and Pascal did not offer it as such. It is an argument that belief is the prudent course for someone who cannot be convinced by evidence. It also does not address which religion to choose, which is the main problem facing it.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does a tiny probability of an infinite reward always outweigh a certain finite cost?
2. Can you decide to believe something by calculating that it would be good to?
3. How does the argument change if more than one god is possible?
4. How is it related to the St. Petersburg paradox?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions use finite but very large rewards, or add the possibility of a god who punishes believers. These alter the decision structure and so alter the conclusion.
