# The Prisoner’s Dilemma

**Category:** economics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Merrill Flood; Melvin Dresher; Albert W. Tucker  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Game developed around 1950; Flood’s RAND report (1952); Tucker’s explanatory story  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Type:** model  
**Themes:** cooperation; dominance; collective action  
**Related experiments:** tragedy-of-the-commons; keynesian-beauty-contest

## 1. The Scenario

Two people independently choose to cooperate or defect. Use illustrative utility payoffs: if both cooperate, each receives 3; if both defect, each receives 1. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector receives 5 and the cooperator 0. Assume each player prefers larger values and cannot make a binding agreement.

For either possible action of the other person, defection gives you a larger payoff. Yet both receive less from mutual defection than from mutual cooperation. The numerical labels are an editorial example; the defining feature is their ordering, not a specific prison sentence or currency.

## 2. The Central Question

How can individually compelling strategic choices produce an outcome each player ranks below another available joint outcome?

## 3. Why It Matters

The game offers a precise model of a cooperation problem. It is useful because its assumptions can be changed systematically: repetition, monitoring, enforceable agreements, or altered motivations may transform the incentives. Whether a real dispute has this structure is an empirical modeling question, not something proved by giving it the game’s name.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**One-shot dominance analysis** recommends defection under the stated utility representation and standard individual decision assumptions. **Repeated-game analysis** asks whether future rewards or retaliation can support cooperation; conclusions depend on horizon, information, and payoff details. **Moral or team reasoning** questions whether individual maximization exhausts rational deliberation. Altering utility to include concern for others may remove the original payoff structure altogether.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

A prisoner’s dilemma is not any disagreement or zero-sum conflict. Mutual cooperation benefits both relative to mutual defection. Communication alone does not create enforceable commitments. A strategy’s success in one repeated-game tournament does not establish a universally optimal strategy against all opponents.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which payoff inequality makes defection dominant?
2. How would an enforceable agreement change the strategic problem?
3. Could a society reshape incentives so that public benefit no longer requires resisting individual temptation?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Iterated games introduce history-dependent strategies. Public-goods and commons models generalize cooperation problems to many participants, but their payoff structures should be checked instead of assumed identical.
