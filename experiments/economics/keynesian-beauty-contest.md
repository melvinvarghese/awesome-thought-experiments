# Keynes’s Beauty Contest

**Category:** economics  
**Associated thinker(s):** John Maynard Keynes  
**Original publication or approximate period:** The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, Chapter 12 (1936)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Type:** model  
**Themes:** expectations; markets; strategic reasoning  
**Related experiments:** prisoners-dilemma; allais-paradox

## 1. The Scenario

A newspaper contest displays many photographs and asks entrants to select a set of faces. The prize goes to a selection that best matches the aggregate choices. Choosing what you personally prefer may be a poor strategy. You instead consider what other people will select, while recognizing that they may also be forecasting the group.

Keynes used the image to illuminate financial speculation: an investor may care about the price others will pay soon rather than an asset’s long-run yield. The contest is an analogy for a pressure within markets, not a complete mathematical theory of every asset price.

## 2. The Central Question

How does a reward for matching collective expectations change what it is rational for an individual to predict?

## 3. Why It Matters

The contest introduces higher-order beliefs: beliefs about other people’s beliefs. It helps explain why having a good estimate of long-run value might not be enough for short-term success. It also suggests that institutions can reward conformity even when some participants privately doubt the prevailing assessment.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The speculative-market reading** emphasizes self-referential expectations and the difficulty of profiting by correcting the crowd too early. **An equilibrium reading** asks which common beliefs and information structures can make forecasts mutually consistent. **Experimental bounded-reasoning approaches** examine how far people actually iterate predictions. A stylized game cannot by itself establish whether a particular market price is a bubble.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Keynes was not recommending judgments of human worth based on appearance; the historical contest supplies a strategic analogy. The familiar “guess two thirds of the average number” game is a later adaptation, not the exact newspaper scenario. Market prices can also depend on dividends, constraints, and information rather than only crowd imitation.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would you change your selection if you knew all other entrants shared your private ranking?
2. When can a belief about what others will believe become self-confirming?
3. What institutional change could reward accurate long-run assessment instead of successful conformity?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Number-guessing games make recursive reasoning explicit. For numbers in a fixed nonnegative interval and a target below the mean, iterated reasoning can push guesses toward zero; actual play depends on beliefs about others’ reasoning.
