# The Stag Hunt

**Category:** economics  
**Type:** model  
**Associated thinker(s):** Jean-Jacques Rousseau  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Rousseau, *Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality* (1755); later formalized in game theory<br>
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** coordination, trust, risk  
**Related experiments:** [The Prisoner’s Dilemma](prisoners-dilemma.md); [The Tragedy of the Commons](tragedy-of-the-commons.md); [The State of Nature](../society-and-justice/state-of-nature.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Two hunters can each choose to hunt a stag or a hare. A stag can only be caught if both hunters cooperate, and it feeds both well. A hare can be caught by one hunter alone, but it is a smaller meal. If one hunter goes after the stag and the other goes after a hare, the stag hunter gets nothing.

An illustrative payoff table is below. Each cell lists the payoffs to Player 1 and Player 2.

| | Player 2 hunts stag | Player 2 hunts hare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Player 1 hunts stag** | 4, 4 | 0, 3 |
| **Player 1 hunts hare** | 3, 0 | 3, 3 |

## 2. The Central Question

When cooperation is best for everyone but risky, what lets people trust each other enough to coordinate?

## 3. Why It Matters

Unlike the Prisoner’s Dilemma, the stag hunt has no dominant strategy: hunting stag is a best response if the other hunter chooses stag, while hunting hare is a best response if the other chooses hare. With the illustrative payoffs above, both hunters choosing stag and both choosing hare are Nash equilibria. The stag equilibrium gives both a higher payoff (4, 4 rather than 3, 3), so it is payoff-dominant. The hare equilibrium is risk-dominant for this payoff table: under an equal-likelihood estimate of the other hunter’s choice, hare yields 3 while stag has an expected payoff of 2. This risk-dominance result depends on the payoffs and the stated belief criterion; it is not true of every game called a stag hunt.

In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, defection is individually dominant even when the other player cooperates, and mutual cooperation is not a Nash equilibrium. In the stag hunt, the best response depends on the other player’s choice, and mutual cooperation is an equilibrium, but a player risks getting nothing by hunting stag alone.

Joining a protest, adopting a shared standard, or investing in a joint project can serve as teaching analogies for coordination problems. They are not predictions made by this two-player model. In a real case, institutions, communication, or reputation might affect expectations, payoffs, or the salience of a coordinated choice; whether they help depends on the details.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Payoff-dominance** favours the stag equilibrium because both hunters do better there. **Risk-dominance** favours the hare equilibrium with the payoffs shown above; changing payoff relations can change which equilibrium is risk-dominant. **Evolutionary analyses** add assumptions about population structure and how strategies change over time, so the basic matrix alone does not determine which outcome a population settles into. Brian Skyrms uses the stag hunt as a model for questions about social contracts; that is a broader interpretation, not a conclusion entailed by the two-player payoff table.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The numbers in the table are illustrative, not part of Rousseau’s original story. In Part II of the *Discourse on Inequality*, Rousseau describes hunters who understand the need to keep their positions when pursuing a deer, but who may leave the hunt to chase a passing hare. That passage is a verbal scenario, not a formal payoff matrix or equilibrium analysis. The formal model depends on the ordering of payoffs. The stag hunt is not a Prisoner’s Dilemma: in the stag hunt the obstacle is coordination under risk, rather than a dominant incentive to defect.

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Many-player versions can require cooperation from a threshold number of participants, or even full cooperation. Their equilibrium and coordination problems can differ from those in the two-person model.
