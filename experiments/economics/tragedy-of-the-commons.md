# The Tragedy of the Commons

**Category:** economics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Garrett Hardin; Elinor Ostrom (major empirical challenge to universalized conclusions)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Hardin, “The Tragedy of the Commons” (1968); earlier commons discussions predate it  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Type:** model  
**Themes:** externalities; resources; institutions  
**Related experiments:** prisoners-dilemma; keynesian-beauty-contest

## 1. The Scenario

Several herders use a pasture. Adding one animal brings its owner most of the additional revenue, while the damage from overgrazing is distributed among everyone. Suppose each herder makes decisions separately, the pasture is finite, and no effective rules limit use. Each may see an incentive to add animals even when the combined expansion damages the resource on which all depend.

The scenario is a model with institutional assumptions. A pasture governed by collective access rules, monitoring, or negotiated limits is not automatically the same case as unrestricted open access.

## 2. The Central Question

When do individual incentives undermine the shared resource needed for everyone’s continued benefit?

## 3. Why It Matters

The pasture directs attention to an externality: a decision maker may not bear the full costs of an action. It is a starting point for comparing institutions, not a demonstration that one ownership arrangement always works. To apply it responsibly, ask who can enter, who can exclude, how damage is measured, and who can enforce agreements.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Hardin’s model** emphasizes incentives toward expansion when private returns and shared costs diverge. **Institutional analysis** asks whether collective rule-making, public regulation, or private rights can alter those incentives. Ostrom’s research is a major reason to resist equating common property with inevitable failure. These alternatives must be compared in actual settings rather than selected by a slogan; the scenario alone cannot rank every governance design.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

“Commonly owned” does not mean “unregulated.” The model does not establish that human beings always act selfishly or that privatization is the only remedy. Hardin’s wider population arguments are separate from the narrow pasture mechanism. A model can clarify one causal pressure without giving a complete history of a real environmental problem.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What minimum rule would make adding an animal less attractive when the pasture is overloaded?
2. Who should monitor use, and who pays for monitoring?
3. How could evidence distinguish an incentive failure from poverty, exclusion, or unequal political power?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Fisheries, groundwater, and atmospheric pollution have related incentive problems but different boundaries and enforcement difficulties. Public-goods games model contribution to a benefit rather than extraction from a resource.
