# Brain Transplant: Brown and Robinson

**Category:** identity  
**Associated thinker(s):** Sydney Shoemaker  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity (1963)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** embodiment, memory, survival  
**Related experiments:** lockes-prince-and-cobbler, fission  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

Suppose a surgical procedure removes two people’s brains for treatment. A mistake places Brown’s brain into Robinson’s body. The survivor, often called Brownson, remembers Brown’s life, recognizes Brown’s friends, and displays Brown’s characteristic attitudes. The familiar body points toward Robinson; the psychology and brain point toward Brown. The imagined procedure is a philosophical stipulation, not a description of feasible surgery.

## 2. The Central Question

Which person, if either, survives when brain and the rest of the body come apart?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case makes psychological continuity vivid while also allowing a material basis for it. It challenges crude appeals to outward appearance but leaves open whether brain continuity matters because of psychology, because of biological organization, or for another reason.

Vary only one feature at a time. First preserve the brain and psychology together. Next imagine preserving the same psychology through a different process. Finally ask what would happen if brain tissue survived but psychology changed drastically. These comparisons distinguish a brain criterion from a psychological criterion instead of treating them as equivalent. Actual biology may constrain the possibilities, and a philosophical conclusion should state which stipulated possibilities it needs. A case that cannot occur can still test concepts, but only if its crucial assumptions are coherent enough to be assessed.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Psychological approaches typically identify Brownson with Brown, provided the relevant causal connection is appropriate. Animalists question whether the original human animal travels with a transplanted cerebrum or remains with its organism. Other accounts distinguish the persistence of persons from that of their bodies or animals.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Following the brain is not automatically an immaterial-soul theory. Conversely, taking biological life seriously need not mean equating identity with skin or facial appearance. Brain, cerebrum, and brainstem variants can have different implications; their details should be stated.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would you follow the whole brain but not a transferred memory pattern?
2. What makes the recipient’s memories genuine rather than merely convincing?
3. Could the answer differ for a person and for a human organism?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Fission places each viable brain half into a different recipient and removes the possibility of a unique psychological continuer. Williams-style body-swap discussions also ask how framing pain and anticipation can affect identity intuitions.
