# Dion and Theon

**Category:** identity  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Associated thinker(s):** Chrysippus (traditionally associated)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Stoic discussion, known through later reports; attribution is traditional  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** coincidence, parts and wholes, ancient metaphysics  
**Related experiments:** [Lumpl and Goliath: The Statue and the Clay](statue-and-clay.md); [Ship of Theseus](ship-of-theseus.md); [The Problem of the Many (Tibbles the Cat)](problem-of-the-many.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Consider Dion, a man with two feet. Now consider Theon, defined as Dion minus his left foot: the whole of Dion except that foot. Suppose Dion’s left foot is amputated. After the amputation, Dion still exists, and he is now missing a foot. Theon, who was just the rest of Dion, is also still there, made of the same matter as Dion now is.

But then before the amputation there seem to be two things, Dion and Theon, which are different in size, and after it, they coincide. A thing seems to have become identical to something it was previously distinct from, or a distinct object has vanished.

## 2. The Central Question

When a part is removed, does the original object survive, and what has become of the object that was the remainder?

## 3. Why It Matters

The puzzle is an early statement of problems about parts and wholes, identity over time and the claim that identity is permanent. It shows how defining an object by subtraction (a thing minus a part) can create apparent objects that we are tempted to count as separate from the original.

It is helpful for evaluating whether our ordinary talk of parts requires that every possible collection of matter be an object.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Rejecting arbitrary parts** says that Theon, as an arbitrary subtraction, is not a genuine object and there is no puzzle. **Four-dimensionalism** treats the two as sharing a stage after the amputation but differing earlier, like roads that merge. **Relative identity and coincidence views** accept that the two were different things that now coincide. **Sortal views** distinguish a man from the body part-collection by the kinds they fall under. These solutions have different consequences for the problem of the many.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The puzzle is not mainly about medicine or amputation, and the foot is chosen only for simplicity. It also does not show that a man ceases to exist when he loses a limb. The ancient attribution is traditional; the particular reasoning of the original authors is known only indirectly.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Do you think Theon exists before the amputation, or is it only a way of talking?
2. Which would you give up: that Dion survives, that Theon survives, or that distinct things cannot coincide?
3. Does the puzzle work if the part removed is a single atom?
4. What does it suggest about how many objects exist in a room?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions that remove a different part or that remove nothing, but only redescribe the object, test whether the puzzle depends on an actual change or only on language. The related lump-and-statue case focuses on coincidence without a change of parts.
