# The Problem of the Many (Tibbles the Cat)

**Category:** identity  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Associated thinker(s):** Peter Unger, Peter Geach  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Unger, “The Problem of the Many” (Midwest Studies in Philosophy, 1980); the cat example is associated with Geach and later discussion  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** vagueness, boundaries, counting objects  
**Related experiments:** [Sorites: When Does a Heap Begin?](../logic/sorites.md); [Lumpl and Goliath: The Statue and the Clay](statue-and-clay.md); [Ship of Theseus](ship-of-theseus.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Tibbles the cat sits on the mat. Look closely at her surface. Some hairs are just about to fall out. Some are loosely attached, some are barely in contact. Where precisely does the cat end? There are very many collections of atoms, differing by a hair or a molecule, each of which is a good candidate for being the cat. Each seems to be a cat-shaped thing with just as good a claim as any other.

If each candidate is a cat, there seem to be thousands of cats on the mat. If only one is, what makes it special?

## 2. The Central Question

How can there be one cat when so many equally good candidate objects occupy nearly the same place?

## 3. Why It Matters

The problem arises for any ordinary object with fuzzy boundaries: clouds, mountains, people. Nothing about it depends on cats. It is a test of any theory of vagueness, composition and counting. We count cats on the mat without difficulty, which suggests that our counting practice tolerates overlap that a strict reading of identity does not.

Responses touch other debates too, including whether an object’s boundaries are metaphysically vague or only semantically imprecise.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Supervaluation and semantic indecision** says that “Tibbles” is vague in what it refers to; each candidate is a permissible referent, and statements true on all sharpenings are true. **Many-cats views** accept the many candidates but say they almost coincide, so counting “by almost-identity” gives one cat. **Ontological sparseness** denies that all those candidates exist as objects. **Vague-objects views** hold that the world itself has no sharp boundary. These disagree on what should be revised.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The problem is not about microscopic measurement, and sharpening the cat’s edge by physics will not obviously solve it, since new candidates arise at any level. It also does not say there are in fact thousands of cats; it asks how to avoid the apparent consequence. Do not confuse it with the sorites, though both involve vagueness.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. How many cats are on the mat, and how do you know?
2. Is the vagueness in the cat, or in the word “cat”?
3. Would a perfectly detailed physical description settle where the cat ends?
4. What does your answer imply for counting people in a crowd or clouds in the sky?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The same structure arises for clouds and mountains. A separate puzzle about a cat that loses its tail concerns parts and persistence rather than vague boundaries; the Stanford Encyclopedia’s entry on identity over time is the place to read it, and the two puzzles should not be merged.
