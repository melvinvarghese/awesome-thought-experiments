# Swampman

**Category:** identity  
**Associated thinker(s):** Donald Davidson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Knowing One’s Own Mind (1987)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** mental content, history, externalism  
**Related experiments:** teletransporter, chinese-room  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

A lightning strike destroys Davidson in a swamp. By an extraordinary coincidence, another physical process makes an exact molecular duplicate from nearby material. The duplicate behaves as Davidson would: it walks home, recognizes familiar-looking people, and produces the same sentences. However, it has no actual history of learning words or interacting with those people. Its apparent memories were not caused by those remembered events.

## 2. The Central Question

Do a thinker’s meanings and thoughts depend partly on a history outside the thinker’s present physical state?

## 3. Why It Matters

The example is centrally about mental content and semantic externalism, although its duplication setup also invites identity questions. It separates present internal structure from the historical and social relations through which thoughts might acquire their subject matter.

Distinguish three questions: is the duplicate the same individual, does it have experiences, and do its words refer to the same things? None follows automatically from an answer to another. A theory might deny personal identity while allowing consciousness and content. Another might allow experience while denying historically grounded reference at the moment of creation. Keeping the questions separate prevents a dramatic duplication narrative from substituting for an argument. It also reveals how an apparently tiny difference in a scenario’s history can become central once mental content is the actual target.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Davidson’s externalist treatment denies that the brand-new duplicate immediately has the same contentful thoughts as the original. Internalists resist this result, emphasizing current organization and cognitive capacities. Some historical theories distinguish having general capacities for experience from possessing particular historically grounded concepts.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Swampman was not introduced simply to ask whether a copy has a soul. Davidson’s target is the relation between content and causal history. Lack of genuine memories does not, without a further argument, establish lack of all consciousness or justify mistreating the duplicate.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Can a perfect behavioral duplicate fail to mean what you mean?
2. What would count as the duplicate acquiring a genuine concept?
3. Would an intentionally manufactured copy differ from a coincidental one?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Twin Earth cases vary the external environment while holding internal features fixed. Swampman instead removes the ordinary causal history altogether, making the role of historical connections especially stark.
