# Ship of Theseus

**Category:** identity  
**Associated thinker(s):** Plutarch, Thomas Hobbes (reassembly variation)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Plutarch, Life of Theseus §23, ancient account; Hobbes’s later addition  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** persistence, parts, identity  
**Related experiments:** teletransporter, lockes-prince-and-cobbler  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

A cherished ship is repaired over many years. As each plank decays, craftspeople replace it. Eventually none of the original timber remains, but the vessel has continued sailing throughout. Is it the original ship? Hobbes sharpens the case: suppose someone saves the discarded planks and later builds a second vessel from them. Continuous history favors one ship; original material favors the other.

## 2. The Central Question

What allows an object to remain the very same object while its parts change?

## 3. Why It Matters

The puzzle distinguishes numerical identity, being one and the same thing, from qualitative similarity. Two ships can look indistinguishable without being one ship. It also shows why a plausible criterion such as material continuity can conflict with a plausible criterion such as continued use.

Label the continuously repaired vessel A and the reconstructed vessel B. If both are declared identical to the original O, ordinary transitivity implies A and B are identical. Since they can sail away in different directions, that result needs explanation. One response revises the initial judgments; another offers a more elaborate account of objects and time. Saying that both are “the same in different senses” may be helpful only if those senses are specified. Authenticity, ownership, historical connection, and strict identity answer different questions and can come apart.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Continuity accounts favor the vessel with an uninterrupted organized history. Material accounts emphasize original components, although they must explain ordinary repairs. Other approaches analyze objects through temporal parts, constitution, or context-sensitive classifications. A satisfactory account must say what happens when the competing claims occur together.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Theseus did not author the philosophical puzzle. Plutarch records an ancient discussion; the reconstructed second ship is a later complication associated with Hobbes. The issue is not simply whether people would continue using the same name, since names can outlast or change independently of objects.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would replacing all the planks at once differ from gradual repair?
2. Can both vessels be authentic without both being numerically identical to the original?
3. Do organisms and artifacts require the same persistence criteria?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Reassembly creates a rival claimant, while ordinary gradual repair does not. Comparisons with restored paintings and living organisms are useful extensions, but their different functions and biological organization may matter.
