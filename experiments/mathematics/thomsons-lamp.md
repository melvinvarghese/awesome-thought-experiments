# Thomson’s Lamp

**Category:** mathematics  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** James F. Thomson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Thomson, “Tasks and Super-Tasks” (Analysis, 1954)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** supertasks, infinity, limits  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](../logic/achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Dichotomy](../logic/zenos-dichotomy.md); [Hilbert’s Hotel](hilberts-hotel.md)

## 1. The Scenario

A lamp is switched on for one minute, then off for half a minute, then on for a quarter of a minute, and so on, with each interval half as long as the previous one. After two minutes, infinitely many switches have been completed.

Is the lamp on or off at the end of the two minutes? It cannot be on, because each time it was switched on, it was later switched off. It cannot be off, because each time it was switched off, it was later switched on.

## 2. The Central Question

Is it possible to complete an infinite sequence of actions in a finite time, and if so, what determines the state at the end?

## 3. Why It Matters

The lamp is a supertask: a task made of infinitely many steps. Thomson used it to argue that supertasks are impossible. The puzzle shows that a description of the infinite sequence of steps does not by itself determine the state after it.

This matters for philosophy of mathematics and physics, for example in discussions of whether space and time are continuous, and whether hypothetical computers could perform infinitely many operations.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Benacerraf’s reply** says that the description of the steps does not settle the final state, so there is no contradiction: the lamp may be on or off. **Thomson’s view** was that the story is impossible. **Physical objections** note that real switches would need to move arbitrarily fast. **Mathematical readings** treat it as a point about limits: a sequence need not determine its limit.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox is not solved by saying that the lamp would burn out, and it does not show that Zeno’s dichotomy is impossible. It shows that the sequence of states does not determine a final state. Also, the existence of a limit of a sequence of numbers does not entail a limit of a sequence of physical states.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does the story describe something logically impossible?
2. What would be needed to fix the state at two minutes?
3. How does this relate to Zeno’s Dichotomy?
4. Could a physical system perform infinitely many operations in finite time?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The Ross–Littlewood urn is a supertask with a different puzzle structure. These are separate cases and require separate analysis.
