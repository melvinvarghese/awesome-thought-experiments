# Thomson’s Lamp

**Category:** mathematics  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** James F. Thomson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Thomson, “Tasks and Super-Tasks” (*Analysis*, 1954)\
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** supertasks, infinity, limits  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](../logic/achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Dichotomy](../logic/zenos-dichotomy.md); [Hilbert’s Hotel](hilberts-hotel.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Start with a lamp off. Press its toggle button after one minute, again after another half-minute, then after another quarter-minute, and continue with each interval halved. The infinitely many presses accumulate at the two-minute mark, with no last press.

Thomson presents a dilemma: the lamp cannot be on, because every switch-on is followed by a later switch-off; it cannot be off, because every switch-off after the first is followed by a later switch-on. Yet the lamp must be one or the other. Benacerraf later challenges the inference to a contradiction: the switching instructions say what happens at each finite stage, but do not specify a separate state at exactly two minutes.

## 2. The Central Question

Can an infinite sequence of actions be completed in finite time, and do the instructions determine the system’s state at the endpoint?

## 3. Why It Matters

A supertask is a task made up of infinitely many steps that is completed in a finite time. Thomson used the lamp to argue that the notion of a completed supertask is unexplained and that the described sequence cannot be completed. Benacerraf’s reply challenges the inference from the switching sequence to a contradiction; it does not prove that a physical lamp can perform the sequence.

The case separates two questions: what the instructions specify at every finite stage, and what—if anything—they specify at the limit time.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Thomson’s argument** treats the incompatible-seeming end-state reasoning as evidence against completing the task. **Benacerraf’s reply** is that either “on” or “off” can be assigned at the terminal instant without contradicting the switching instructions alone. That reply does not show that a supertask is physically possible; it says the described sequence does not itself settle the endpoint.

The alternating sequence of binary lamp states has no ordinary real-valued limit. The SEP discussion notes that Cesàro averaging can assign it a mean of one-half; that mean does not tell us whether the binary lamp is on or off. Physical implementations raise additional questions beyond this mathematical description.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Saying that an actual lamp would burn out changes the idealized setup rather than resolving its question. The lamp is also different from Zeno’s Dichotomy: the latter uses a convergent sequence of distances, while the lamp’s alternating binary states have no ordinary limit. A convergent total time for the intervals does not, by itself, assign a lamp state at the endpoint.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does the description make a completed infinite sequence logically impossible?
2. What additional rule would fix the lamp’s state at two minutes?
3. How is the lamp different from Zeno’s Dichotomy?
4. What would a physical realization need to specify beyond the sequence of switchings?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The Ross–Littlewood urn is another supertask, but it uses a different sequence of operations and raises a different question about the final state.
