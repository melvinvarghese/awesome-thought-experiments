# Hilbert’s Hotel

**Category:** mathematics  
**Associated thinker(s):** David Hilbert; George Gamow  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Hilbert’s lectures (1924–25); popularized by Gamow (1947)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Themes:** infinity; cardinality; bijections  
**Related experiments:** st-petersburg-paradox; bertrands-paradox

## 1. The Scenario

A hotel has rooms numbered by all positive integers: 1, 2, 3, and so on, with no last room. Every room is occupied. A new guest arrives. Ask each existing guest in room n to move to room n + 1. Room 1 is now vacant and no existing guest lacks a room.

A bus carrying countably infinitely many guests can also be accommodated. Move each existing guest from n to 2n and give the arriving guest numbered n room 2n − 1. Each old guest has a distinct even-numbered room, and each newcomer has a distinct odd-numbered one.

## 2. The Central Question

Why can an infinite collection have the same cardinality as a proper part of itself?

## 3. Why It Matters

The scenario makes a definition visible. Two sets have the same cardinality when their elements can be paired one-to-one, even if one set is a proper subset of the other. Finite intuitions about “full” combine different ideas: every place is occupied, and no rearrangement can create a vacancy. Those ideas come apart for infinite sets.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Set-theoretic interpretation:** the room maps are explicit bijections or injections demonstrating countable cardinality. There is no contradiction in standard infinite-set mathematics. **Physical interpretation:** whether an actual universe could contain such a hotel involves additional claims about matter, space, time, and possibility. Mathematical coherence alone neither constructs a real hotel nor proves every proposed physical infinity possible.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

There is no final guest who falls off the end. Moving guests successively is not needed for the mathematical mapping to be well-defined. The example does not show that every infinity is the same size; some sets cannot be listed by positive integers. It also does not make ordinary subtraction of infinite quantities behave like subtraction of finite numbers.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Why does n → n + 1 leave exactly one empty room rather than displace the last guest?
2. Design a room assignment for three countably infinite buses arriving together.
3. Which extra assumptions would turn the mathematical assignment into a physically executable process?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Countably many buses can be handled by enumerating pairs of positive integers. Cantor’s diagonal argument shows why a hotel indexed by positive integers cannot house every member of an uncountable set.
