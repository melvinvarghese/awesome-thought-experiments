# Sleeping Beauty

**Category:** mathematics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Adam Elga; Robert Stalnaker; Arnold Zuboff  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Canonical published discussion: Elga, “Self-locating belief and the Sleeping Beauty problem” (2000); earlier precursors  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Themes:** self-location; credence; sampling  
**Related experiments:** monty-hall; st-petersburg-paradox

## 1. The Scenario

Before sleeping, Beauty learns the protocol. A fair coin determines her awakenings. Heads produces one awakening, on Monday. Tails produces two, on Monday and Tuesday. After each interview, a treatment removes her memory of that interview. At every interview she knows the protocol but does not know the day.

Upon awakening she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. The uncertainty is partly about the world and partly about her location within it. A tails world contains two subjectively indistinguishable interviews, whereas a heads world contains only one.

## 2. The Central Question

Should Beauty assign heads probability 1/2 or 1/3 when interviewed?

## 3. Why It Matters

The question tests how ordinary conditionalization should handle self-locating information. A fair coin defines a distribution over trials. A collection of interviews has a different frequency structure. Translating between them requires a principle about what evidence the agent has and how her present perspective is represented.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Halfers** emphasize that Beauty knew she would awaken whichever result occurred and may regard the awakening as no new evidence about the toss. **Thirders** emphasize the three possible centered situations: heads-Monday, tails-Monday, and tails-Tuesday; Elga argues for equal probabilities and hence 1/3 for heads. Further positions distinguish kinds of credence or updating. Merely pointing out the coin’s physical fairness does not settle the philosophical disagreement.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

No one needs to claim that the coin becomes physically biased. Counting interviews does not automatically settle what one should believe about a randomly selected trial. Betting arguments depend on whether stakes are paid per interview, per experiment, or under some other rule; changing that rule can change the decision problem.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What exactly has Beauty learned when she notices that she is awake?
2. Should doubling the number of tails interviews change a credence about the toss?
3. How would you design a bet that makes its payment schedule completely explicit?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Variants change the coin bias, number of awakenings, or whether Beauty is told the day. These changes help isolate whether the disputed update concerns possible worlds or positions within worlds.
