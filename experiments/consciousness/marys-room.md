# Mary’s Room: The Knowledge Argument

**Category:** consciousness  
**Associated thinker(s):** Frank Jackson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Epiphenomenal Qualia (1982); What Mary Didn’t Know (1986)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** knowledge, experience, physicalism  
**Related experiments:** zombies, inverted-spectrum  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

Mary is an idealized scientist who has lived entirely in a black-and-white environment. She knows every physical fact about human color vision: what light does, how brains process it, and how people describe colors. Eventually she sees red for the first time. The premise is not merely that she has read a great many textbooks; she is granted exhaustive physical knowledge, however unrealistic that is as a biography.

## 2. The Central Question

If Mary learns something upon seeing red, does that show that physical knowledge leaves out facts about consciousness?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument makes a possible gap between description and experience explicit. Its crucial inference is from gaining knowledge to discovering a fact not included among the physical facts. That step, rather than the vivid room alone, carries the metaphysical burden.

One helpful reconstruction asks three separate questions: did Mary lack an experience, did she lack knowledge, and did she lack knowledge of a fact not already among the physical facts? Agreement on the first question does not settle the last. A person can acquire a skill or a new recognitional concept without the underlying world gaining a new property. Whether that analogy works for phenomenal knowledge is precisely what must be defended. State the relevant sense of “knows” whenever moving between descriptions, abilities, direct acquaintance, and factual understanding.

## 4. Major Interpretations

The antiphysicalist response says Mary learns a previously unknown phenomenal fact. The ability hypothesis says she instead acquires capacities such as recognizing or imagining red. Acquaintance and new-concept responses distinguish a new way of knowing from a new fact. Physicalists may also contest whether the exhaustive-knowledge premise can be adequately imagined.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Mary is not simply color-blind. Her initial lack of colored experience is stipulated while her future ability to see color is preserved. Learning something does not by itself prove dualism; the nature of what is learned must be established. Jackson later rejected his original antiphysicalist conclusion.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Can you gain knowledge without learning a new fact?
2. Would knowing every physical detail enable imagination of an unfamiliar experience?
3. What must an ability account explain about Mary’s first judgment?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Jackson also discusses Fred, who discriminates colors unavailable to ordinary observers. Mary variants change the timing and kind of knowledge, but must preserve the exhaustive-physical-knowledge condition if they are to test the original argument.
