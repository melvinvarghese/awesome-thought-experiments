# Mad Pain and Martian Pain

**Category:** consciousness  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** David Lewis  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Lewis, “Mad Pain and Martian Pain” (in Block, ed., Readings in Philosophy of Psychology, 1980)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** functionalism, identity theory, multiple realizability  
**Related experiments:** [Inverted Spectrum](inverted-spectrum.md); [Philosophical Zombies](zombies.md); [China Brain](china-brain.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Take two odd creatures. The first is a madman who feels something just like our pain, but whose pain has strange causes and effects: it is brought on by moderate exercise, it helps him concentrate on mathematics, and it makes him cross his legs and snap his fingers instead of groaning and withdrawing. The second is a Martian whose physiology is nothing like ours. The state in her that is caused by injury and that makes her wince is a hydraulic state, not a state of nerves as in us.

Both, by hypothesis, are in a state that we are inclined to call pain, yet each departs from the human pattern in a different way.

## 2. The Central Question

Can one theory of what pain is accommodate both a being whose pain plays an unusual causal role and a being whose pain has a different physical basis?

## 3. Why It Matters

Two major theories of mind pull in opposite directions. An identity theory ties pain to a type of brain state, which seems to exclude the Martian. A functionalist theory ties pain to a causal role, which seems to exclude the madman. Lewis proposed a way to give the theory both a role and a realizer relative to a species or population.

The cases test how closely mental concepts are tied to physical structure, and whether there are limits to what could count as a mind.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Species-relative identity views** say pain is whatever state occupies the pain role in a given kind of creature. **Strict functionalism** says pain is the role itself, with the madman as a case where the role is atypically filled. **Conservative views** deny that either creature is really in pain. Each answer has costs: for example, in how it generalizes to artificial systems.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The cases do not demonstrate that Martians exist, nor do they show that functionalism is true. They illustrate a conflict among intuitive conditions. It would also be wrong to say that the madman has no pain simply because his pain lacks the usual causes; that is the kind of judgment under dispute.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which of the two creatures is easier to count as being in pain, and why?
2. Does it matter whether the Martian’s pain feels the way ours does?
3. What would you need to know to decide whether a robot is in pain?
4. Can a theory of pain be correct for humans but fail for other species?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The cases extend naturally to artificial systems. Chinese-nation and fading-qualia scenarios explore related questions about whether organization alone is enough.
