# Split Brains and the Unity of Consciousness

**Category:** consciousness  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Thomas Nagel  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Nagel, “Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness” (Synthese, 1971)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** unity of consciousness, split brain, number of minds  
**Related experiments:** [Fission and Personal Identity](../identity/fission.md); [What Is It Like to Be a Bat?](bat.md); [Brain Transplant: Brown and Robinson](../identity/brain-transplant.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In some patients, surgeons have cut the main connection between the two halves of the brain to control severe epilepsy. In laboratory settings, a patient can be shown a word to one half of the visual field and respond with the hand controlled by the corresponding hemisphere, while the other hemisphere and hand respond differently. In everyday life such patients usually behave normally.

Nagel asks how to describe what is going on. Is there one subject with a divided mind, two subjects in one body, or something that does not fit either answer?

## 2. The Central Question

How many minds, or streams of consciousness, are there in a patient whose brain has been divided?

## 3. Why It Matters

We normally assume that one body has one stream of experience, so the natural question for any case is “how many?” Nagel argues that the split-brain data may resist every straightforward answer, so that our ordinary concept of a single unified mind may not be well defined across such cases.

This is a clinical-based case, not a purely imaginary one, which makes it important evidence for theories of consciousness and personal identity. It also supports the use of imaginary variants that extend it.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**One-mind views** say there is one subject with disunified access. **Two-minds views** say each hemisphere supports a subject. **Switching views** hold that the patient’s consciousness shifts. **Nagel’s own suggestion** is that the question has no determinate answer in current concepts. Empirical researchers also dispute how much of the early findings generalize.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The case does not show that ordinary people have two minds, and it does not follow that the left and right hemispheres are separate people in everyone. The experimental results were obtained under special conditions and later work has refined them, so this is a conceptual discussion and not a summary of current neuroscience.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What would you need to know about the patient to decide whether there are two subjects?
2. If a patient’s two hemispheres each remember a different part of the day, is that one or two memories?
3. Does the idea of a single self need more than the unity of experience?
4. How does this case differ from imagining a person who divides in two?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Extensions imagine cutting and then reuniting the connection, or performing the operation on healthy people. Those variants are speculative and should be distinguished from the clinical findings.
