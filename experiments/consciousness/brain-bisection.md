## 1. The Scenario

Some people with severe, medication-resistant epilepsy have undergone partial or complete surgery to sever the corpus callosum. Several classic operations also severed other cerebral commissures. In controlled tests, information flashed to one visual field may be processed primarily by the opposite hemisphere and available to the hand it predominantly controls, even when speech or the other hand does not reveal the same information. The exact pattern depends on the patient, the extent of disconnection, the task, and the response being measured. See the primary reports by [Gazzaniga, Bogen, and Sperry (1962)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.48.10.1765) and [Sperry (1968)](https://people.uncw.edu/puente/sperry/sperrypapers/60s/135-1968.pdf).

Patients can show little obvious disruption in ordinary social behavior. But those everyday observations are largely anecdotal; they do not establish that patients are unaffected in daily life. A 2020 review summarizes both evidence for disconnection and evidence for remaining integration, and says current findings do not settle whether consciousness is unified or split ([de Haan et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7305066/)).

Nagel asks how to describe the patient’s experience. Is there one subject with divided access, two streams of experience, partial unity, or something that does not fit these options?

## 2. The Central Question

What do experimental dissociations and everyday behavioral integration tell us about the number and unity of conscious streams in a split-brain patient?

## 3. Why It Matters

Nagel’s 1971 argument weighs the strong integration visible in ordinary behavior against the dissociations elicited in controlled experiments. He argues that these bodies of evidence do not support a straightforward whole-number count of minds, and that the case exposes limits in ordinary ideas of a single subject of experience. This is Nagel’s philosophical conclusion, not a settled neuroscientific result. Read the [paper](https://www.mcgill.ca/science/files/science/channels/attach/brainbisection.pdf) alongside the current [SEP discussion of split-brain models](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-unity/).

The case has shaped debates about phenomenal unity and subjecthood. The experimental findings alone do not decide questions about a person’s identity over time. Variants such as reconnecting a divided brain are philosophical extensions, not clinical evidence.

## 4. Major Interpretations

The main proposals include **two-stream views**, **single-stream views** (including a switch model), **partial-stream views**, and hybrids that treat laboratory and everyday settings differently. The [SEP overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-unity/) explains that these models make different claims about the structure of experience; choosing a model does not by itself settle whether the patient remains one subject.

Nagel’s own 1971 argument is distinct: he contends that ordinary concepts of mind and person do not yield a determinate one-or-two answer for these cases. Philosophers and researchers continue to disagree about how the evidence should be interpreted.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The case does not show that ordinary people have two minds, or that each hemisphere is a separate person. The classic findings come from controlled tests, and patients differ in the extent and type of disconnection. In a 2017 study of two patients with full callosotomy, Pinto and colleagues found that both could detect and locate stimuli presented across the visual field regardless of response mode. In a separate cross-hemifield comparison task with Patient DDC, performance was not significantly above chance, consistent with a failure to integrate the two visual inputs. The authors argued that their broader results support undivided consciousness; this interpretation is contested, not a settled refutation of earlier dissociation findings ([Pinto et al., 2017](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/140/5/1231/2951052)). The broader empirical and philosophical disagreement is reviewed by [de Haan et al. (2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7305066/).

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What evidence would help decide whether there are one or two subjects?
2. If a patient’s hemispheres have access to different information, does that imply two streams of experience?
3. Does the idea of a single self require unified experience?
4. How does this clinical case differ from imagining one person dividing into two?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

One can imagine reconnecting a divided brain or performing the operation on healthy people. These are speculative thought experiments, not established procedures or findings. They should be kept separate from the clinical cases.
