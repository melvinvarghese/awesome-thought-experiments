# Newcomb’s Problem

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Associated thinker(s):** William Newcomb, Robert Nozick  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Formulated by Newcomb; published by Nozick in “Newcomb’s Problem and Two Principles of Choice” (1969)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** decision theory, prediction, causation  
**Related experiments:** [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md); [The Unexpected Hanging / Surprise Test](../logic/unexpected-hanging.md)

## 1. The Scenario

A transparent box contains $1,000. An opaque box contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. Yesterday, a highly reliable predictor filled the opaque box if it predicted you would take only that box, and left it empty if it predicted you would take both. You now choose the opaque box alone or both boxes. The contents are already fixed.

The predictor’s reliability is part of the setup, but perfect reliability is not required for many versions. You prefer more money. The challenge is deciding how to evaluate actions that are strongly associated with different contents while apparently unable to cause those past contents.

## 2. The Central Question

Should you take only the opaque box or take both when your decision is highly predictive of an already fixed reward?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case distinguishes causal influence from evidence. It also exposes ambiguity in the instruction to maximize expected utility: different accounts use different probabilities to evaluate the same act. Debates about free choice sometimes enter the discussion, but the central issue is a conflict between decision principles.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Two-boxing:** for each fixed content of the opaque box, taking both adds $1,000. Causal decision approaches emphasize that the present act does not fill yesterday’s box.

**One-boxing:** given the stipulated predictive connection, choosing only the opaque box is strong evidence that it contains the million. Evidential approaches evaluate the option using that correlation.

**Model-sensitive response:** specifying the predictor, causal structure, and what counts as an available intervention can clarify which probabilities are appropriate. Merely restating “maximize expected value” does not settle their interpretation.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

One-boxers need not claim to change the past. Two-boxers need not deny that past one-boxers received more money in the stipulated record. A comparison of outcomes across differently predicted people differs from comparing acts while holding a particular box content fixed. Also, do not secretly let the predictor refill the box after the choice.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which facts are held fixed in your comparison of the two actions?
2. How much reliability would make the evidential expected payoff favor one-boxing?
3. Would seeing the opaque box’s contents remove the dilemma?
4. What distinguishes a good decision rule from a decision that happens to earn more money?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Transparent-box, imperfect-predictor, and medical common-cause variants probe different assumptions. Their recommendations can differ because the information and causal model differ. Treating them all as exactly the same problem prevents careful diagnosis.
