# The Idle Argument

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Ancient Greek fatalist tradition (the argument’s original author is uncertain); Chrysippus (anciently reported reply)<br>
**Original publication or approximate period:** The argument is known through later reports, including Cicero, *On Fate* 28–30 (composed c. 44 BCE); Aristotle discusses a related consequence in *On Interpretation* 9<br>
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** fatalism, action, fate  
**Related experiments:** [Aristotle’s Sea Battle](sea-battle.md); [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

You are ill. Either you will recover from this illness or you will not. If you will recover, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor. If you will not recover, you will not recover whether or not you call a doctor. So calling a doctor makes no difference. By the same reasoning, no effort of yours makes a difference to anything.

The conclusion, that action is idle, seems absurd, yet each step looks plausible.

## 2. The Central Question

If the future is already fixed, does it follow that nothing we do matters?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument shows how a thesis about what will happen can seem to undermine reasons for acting. Here, fatalism is the thought that an outcome will occur regardless of what we do. Causal determinism, by contrast, says that events—including our actions—are fixed by prior causes; it does not by itself make actions causally ineffective.

It also invites careful thought about conditional claims. “If I will recover, I will recover whether or not I call” sounds harmless, but it may hide an assumption that the outcome is independent of the call.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The Stoic reply**, attributed to Chrysippus by Cicero, is that some outcomes are fated together with the actions needed to bring them about: a recovery may be co-fated with consulting a doctor. That rejects the inference from “the outcome is fated” to “the outcome will happen whether or not I act.” Cicero also presents a version framed in terms of a statement’s having been true from eternity rather than fate. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the generalized version raises a further question about exactly where the argument fails; the co-fated reply should not be presented as settling every version.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument does not require belief in the stars or in destiny; Cicero reports both a fate-framed version and a version about what has been true from eternity. The surviving reports do not identify a secure original author for the named Idle Argument. Nor does determinism, by itself, imply that action is pointless, since actions can remain causally effective. Aristotle’s discussion of deliberation in *On Interpretation* 9 is related background, not evidence that he named or originated this later-reported argument.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Where does the argument first go wrong, if it does?
2. What is the difference between “whatever will be will be” and “whatever will be is independent of what I do”?
3. Could a belief in fate be rational and still lead to action?
4. Does the structure remind you of other arguments about foreknowledge?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Examples include crops that grow regardless of farming or exams passed regardless of study. In each, the question is whether the claimed independence follows from the original premise.
