# Nietzsche’s Eternal Recurrence

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Friedrich Nietzsche  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Nietzsche, The Gay Science, section 341 (1882); also Thus Spoke Zarathustra  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** affirmation, meaning, repetition  
**Related experiments:** [Experience Machine](../ethics/experience-machine.md); [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In a passage of The Gay Science, Nietzsche imagines a demon who visits you in your loneliest moment and tells you that you will have to live this life again, exactly as you have lived it, an immeasurable number of times, with every pain and joy and every small thing in the same sequence.

How would you respond? Would you throw yourself down and curse the demon, or would you answer that you have never heard anything more divine?

## 2. The Central Question

How would you feel about your life if you had to live it again without change, and what does your answer show about how you value it?

## 3. Why It Matters

The scenario works as a test of affirmation. It asks whether you could endorse your life as a whole, including its suffering, rather than wishing it were different. This makes it a powerful tool for thinking about meaning, regret, and the difference between preferring a good life and loving the one you have.

It stands out from many thought experiments because its aim is existential rather than a theory of knowledge or morality.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The existential reading** takes the idea as a test, not a claim about the universe. **The cosmological reading** takes Nietzsche to propose that time really is cyclical. **The ethical reading** ties the test to Nietzsche’s ideal of amor fati, loving one’s fate. Scholars disagree about how these fit together and about how much weight to give notebooks versus published works.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The thought experiment does not assert that you will live again. It is not the same as a physical theory of recurrence, nor as the recurrence of Poincaré. It also does not say that life is worth repeating, but asks how you would react. Nietzsche presents it through a character, so attributing a single definite doctrine to him is contested.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would you welcome the demon’s message? What does your answer reveal?
2. Does it matter that you would not remember your earlier lives?
3. Is this a test of attitude or a claim about reality?
4. What would you need to change now to answer differently?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions ask how you would react if only part of your life repeated, or if it repeated with one change. They test which parts of your life carry the weight of your answer.
