# Nietzsche’s Eternal Recurrence

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Friedrich Nietzsche  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Nietzsche, The Gay Science, section 341 (1882); also Thus Spoke Zarathustra  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** affirmation, meaning, repetition  
**Related experiments:** [Experience Machine](../ethics/experience-machine.md); [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In a passage of The Gay Science, Nietzsche imagines a demon who visits you in your loneliest moment and tells you that you will have to live this life again, exactly as you have lived it, an immeasurable number of times, with every pain and joy and every small thing in the same sequence.

How would you respond? Would you throw yourself down and curse the demon, or would you answer that you have never heard anything more divine?

## 2. The Central Question

How would you feel about your life if you had to live it again without change, and what does your answer show about how you value it?

## 3. Why It Matters

The passage works as a test of affirmation. It asks whether you could endorse your life as a whole, including its suffering, rather than wishing it were different. This makes it a useful prompt for thinking about meaning, regret, and the difference between preferring a good life and willing this life again.

This page emphasizes the scenario’s existential force: how the possibility of recurrence changes the weight of a life. That emphasis does not settle whether Nietzsche also advances recurrence as a cosmological doctrine, or how the thought belongs to his broader ethical outlook.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Three readings are often distinguished, though they need not be mutually exclusive. **The existential reading** emphasizes the demon’s challenge as a test of whether one can affirm one’s life; R. Lanier Anderson’s Stanford Encyclopedia discussion describes §341 in these terms. **The cosmological reading** treats recurrence as a claim about the world’s structure. Neil Sinhababu (2024) argues that an unpublished notebook fragment offers a cosmological argument, while reading *Zarathustra* as fiction that foregrounds the character’s response. **The ethical or affirmative reading** connects willing recurrence with Nietzsche’s ideal of life-affirmation, sometimes discussed alongside *amor fati*. The published works present these themes in different forms: *The Gay Science* §341 stages the demon’s hypothetical; *Beyond Good and Evil* §56 praises the person who wishes what was and is to recur; and *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* dramatizes recurrence through a vision and dialogue. These interpretations and the weight given to notebooks versus published works remain contested; this page does not adjudicate them.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The demon’s speech in *The Gay Science* §341 is hypothetical and does not by itself prove that recurrence happens. It would be too broad, however, to conclude from that passage alone that Nietzsche nowhere presents recurrence as more than a test: *Beyond Good and Evil* §56 and *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* contain more affirmative and cosmological-sounding formulations. How literally or systematically to read these published passages remains contested. Nietzsche’s recurrence is also distinct from Poincaré recurrence as a mathematical result in dynamical systems.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would you welcome the demon’s message? What does your answer reveal?
2. Does it matter that you would not remember your earlier lives?
3. Is this a test of attitude or a claim about reality?
4. What would you need to change now to answer differently?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions ask how you would react if only part of your life repeated, or if it repeated with one change. They test which parts of your life carry the weight of your answer.
