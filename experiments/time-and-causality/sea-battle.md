# Aristotle’s Sea Battle

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Associated thinker(s):** Aristotle  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Aristotle, De Interpretatione, chapter 9 (fourth century BCE); its interpretation is disputed  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** future contingents, fatalism, truth and time  
**Related experiments:** [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md); [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md); [The Idle Argument](idle-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Suppose someone says today, “There will be a sea battle tomorrow.” Either the sentence is true or it is false. If it is true now, then it seems that the battle is already settled and cannot fail to occur. If it is false now, the battle cannot occur. Either way, whether there is a battle seems to be necessary and beyond anyone’s power to change, and deliberation about it is pointless.

Aristotle’s discussion of this case is brief and has been read in many ways.

## 2. The Central Question

Can statements about contingent future events be true or false now without making the future fixed?

## 3. Why It Matters

The puzzle links logic, time and freedom. It asks whether the principle that every statement is true or false (bivalence) applies to the open future. If it does, it seems to threaten the openness of the future. If it does not, ordinary logic must be revised.

It also bears on the semantics of tense and on arguments about fatalism, determinism and planning.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Three-valued or truth-value gap views**, often attributed to Aristotle on one reading, say that future contingents are neither true nor false yet. **Supervaluationist and branching-time views** say they are true or false only relative to how things go. **Ockhamist views** say the sentence is already true or false, but its truth is not a necessity, since it depends on what will happen. **Peircean views** say all “will” statements about contingent events are false.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The puzzle is not a claim that the future is determined by logic. The step from “it is true now that p” to “necessarily p” is the contested one. Nor is Aristotle’s own view clear, since scholars disagree about whether he accepts a truth-value gap.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does saying “it is true that a battle will occur” fix the future in any sense?
2. If you accept truth-value gaps, what happens to the law of excluded middle?
3. How would you describe the difference between a future being settled and being predicted?
4. Does your answer depend on what you think about causal determinism?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Modern versions ask about statements concerning free decisions, or involve foreknowledge by an infallible being. They bring in theological premises, which should be listed separately.
