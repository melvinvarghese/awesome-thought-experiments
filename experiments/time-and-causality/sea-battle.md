# Aristotle’s Sea Battle

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** puzzle  
**Associated thinker(s):** Aristotle  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Aristotle, De Interpretatione, chapter 9 (fourth century BCE); its interpretation is disputed  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** future contingents, fatalism, truth and time  
**Related experiments:** [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md); [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md); [The Idle Argument](idle-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Suppose someone says today, “There will be a sea battle tomorrow.” Either the sentence is true or it is false. If it is true now, then it seems that the battle is already settled and cannot fail to occur. If it is false now, the battle cannot occur. Either way, whether there is a battle seems to be necessary and beyond anyone’s power to change, and deliberation about it is pointless.

Aristotle’s discussion of this case is brief and has been read in many ways.

## 2. The Central Question

Can statements about contingent future events be true or false now without making the future fixed?

## 3. Why It Matters

The puzzle links logic, time and freedom. It asks whether every statement about a contingent future must already be true or false (bivalence), and how that question relates to excluded middle (that either p or not-p). On one influential reconstruction, bivalence together with assumptions about past truth and necessity threatens an open future. Aristotle's closing distinction preserves that a sea battle either will or will not occur without making either outcome necessary. Rejecting bivalence is one response, but it is not the only way to resist the fatalist inference.

It also bears on the semantics of tense and on arguments about fatalism, determinism and planning.

## 4. Major Interpretations

These are later semantic frameworks for handling future contingents, not positions Aristotle explicitly names. **Three-valued or truth-value-gap views** treat future contingents as neither true nor false yet (or assign them a third, “undetermined” value); this is one disputed interpretation of Aristotle. **Branching-time semantics** evaluates claims against possible histories. In **supervaluationism**, a future contingent is neither true nor false at a moment when histories disagree, although its disjunction with its negation can still be true. In **Prior's Ockhamist model**, a future statement can be true relative to a particular history without being settled at the branching moment; this differs from William of Ockham's own view that future contingents may be true or false, though their truth is unknowable to humans but known by God. **Prior's Peircean model** counts a future claim as true only if its outcome holds across all possible histories, so genuinely contingent “will p” and “will not p” claims are both false.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The puzzle is not a claim that the future is determined by logic. The fatalist's move from a future statement's truth (or falsity) now to the event's necessity (or impossibility) is contested; the classical argument also relies on assumptions about past truth. Bivalence and excluded middle are distinct, and disputing the first need not mean rejecting the second. Aristotle's own account remains disputed: scholars disagree about whether he denies bivalence for future contingents or instead restricts a related rule about contradictory pairs.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does saying “it is true that a battle will occur” fix the future in any sense?
2. If you accept truth-value gaps, what happens to the law of excluded middle?
3. How would you describe the difference between a future being settled and being predicted?
4. Does your answer depend on what you think about causal determinism?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Modern versions ask about statements concerning free decisions, or involve foreknowledge by an infallible being. They bring in theological premises, which should be listed separately.
