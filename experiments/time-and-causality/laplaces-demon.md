# Laplace’s Demon

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Pierre-Simon Laplace  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Essai philosophique sur les probabilités (1814; later editions)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** determinism, prediction, laws of nature  
**Related experiments:** [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md); [Newcomb’s Problem](newcomb.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Imagine an intelligence that knows the complete physical condition of the universe and all relevant forces, with enough mathematical capacity to analyze them. In Laplace’s idealized picture, the future and the past would be available to this intelligence without uncertainty.

The familiar label “demon” is a later name for the intelligence, not a claim about a supernatural creature in a laboratory. The example joins a physical assumption about lawful evolution to strong epistemic and computational idealizations. Keeping those ingredients distinct is essential: a world’s history could be fixed even if no inhabitant could calculate it.

## 2. The Central Question

Would complete physical information and unlimited analysis make the future predictable, and what would that imply about human action?

## 3. Why It Matters

The picture clarifies several different claims: that events are determined, that we can predict them, and that choices do not matter. Only the first concerns whether the laws and a complete state permit more than one continuation. A decision can remain causally important even if it is determined.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Determinist idealization:** for an appropriate deterministic physical model, a complete state and laws specify the subsequent evolution. The intelligence dramatizes this commitment.

**Limits-of-prediction response:** chaotic sensitivity, incomplete information, and computational limits can prevent practical forecasting without introducing multiple lawful futures from the exact same state.

**Free-will responses:** incompatibilists see determination as threatening an important kind of freedom; compatibilists argue that acting through one’s reasons and capacities can still count as free. Prediction alone does not settle that dispute.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Chaos is not synonymous with indeterminism. Determinism is not fatalism: the result can depend on what you do, even if what you do has antecedent causes. Modern physics also cannot be summarized as “quantum mechanics simply disproved determinism”; interpretations and different theories handle determination differently.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Could a deterministic system be unpredictable to every observer inside it?
2. Which information would your proposed predictor actually need?
3. If a forecast changes your decision, must the initial forecast have omitted a relevant interaction?
4. What kind of control would you think remains possible if the future is fixed?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Self-referential prediction adds the problem of an agent responding to its forecast. Quantum and relativistic theories change the relevant physical assumptions. These extensions require explicit models; the nineteenth-century image should not be used as a substitute for current physical analysis.
