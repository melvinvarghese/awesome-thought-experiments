# Pereboom’s Four-Case Argument

**Category:** time-and-causality  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Derk Pereboom  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Pereboom, Living Without Free Will (2001)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** manipulation, determinism, moral responsibility  
**Related experiments:** [Frankfurt Cases](frankfurt-cases.md); [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md); [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Professor Plum kills Ms. White for a selfish reason. Pereboom describes four versions of the story.

In Case 1, neuroscientists manipulate Plum’s brain directly, as if by remote control, to produce his reasoning and decision. In Case 2, Plum is programmed at the beginning of his life so that his reasoning will eventually produce the act. In Case 3, Plum’s upbringing and training shaped him in ways that he did not choose and that lead to the act. In Case 4, Plum’s act results from ordinary deterministic laws and the state of the universe long before his birth.

In each case, Plum’s reasoning is rational and responsive to reasons.

## 2. The Central Question

If Plum is not responsible in Case 1, is there any relevant difference that would make him responsible in Case 4?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument targets compatibilism, which says that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism. It starts from a case where almost everyone agrees that Plum is not responsible, and moves in steps to a case that is simply the deterministic world. If no step introduces a relevant difference, then responsibility is not compatible with determinism.

It is a central example of a manipulation argument and has shaped the debate over what compatibilists need to say about manipulated agents.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Hard-line compatibilists** hold that Plum is responsible even in Case 1, given the right psychological conditions. **Soft-line compatibilists** point to a difference between the cases, such as the history of how the agent’s desires were formed. **Incompatibilists** accept Pereboom’s conclusion. **Skeptics about responsibility** use it to support rejecting desert-based blame.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument does not assume that determinism is true. It concerns what follows if it is. Nor does it show that no one is ever responsible: it concerns a specific kind of moral responsibility tied to desert. The cases have been revised in response to critics.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is Plum responsible in Case 1? If not, why not?
2. Which step to Case 4 would you challenge?
3. Does a history of manipulation differ from a history of upbringing?
4. Does this argument rely on intuitions, and how reliable are they?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Other manipulation arguments use different cases, such as Mele’s Ann and Beth. They test the same question with different details, and the replies can differ.
