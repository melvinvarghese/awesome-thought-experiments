# Pereboom’s Four-Case Argument

**Category:** time-and-causality
<br>
**Type:** argument
<br>
**Associated thinker(s):** Derk Pereboom
<br>
**Original publication or approximate period:** Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will* (2001)
<br>
**Difficulty:** Advanced
<br>
**Themes:** manipulation, determinism, moral responsibility<br>
**Related experiments:** [Frankfurt Cases](frankfurt-cases.md); [The Consequence Argument](consequence-argument.md); [Laplace’s Demon](laplaces-demon.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Professor Plum murders Ms. White for personal advantage. Pereboom varies the causal history across four cases while stipulating compatibilist-friendly capacities such as rational, reasons-responsive deliberation. The sequence is summarized in the [archived SEP discussion](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/compatibilism/supplement.html) and analyzed by [Jeppsson (2020)](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1303683/FULLTEXT03).

- **Case 1:** Neuroscientists use radio-like technology to manipulate Plum’s decisions and actions moment by moment, leading him to murder. Pereboom’s setup is intended to preserve reasons-responsive deliberation.
- **Case 2:** Neuroscientists program Plum at birth to become fairly egoistic; when circumstances offer substantial personal gain, he is determined to murder Ms. White.
- **Case 3:** A community raises Plum to be rationally egoistic; when circumstances offer substantial gain, he is determined to murder.
- **Case 4:** Determinism is true. Plum is an ordinary person raised normally, and his action follows from the deterministic causal history. The argument treats him as having compatibilist-friendly capacities such as reasons-responsiveness.

Pereboom argues that the move from the manipulated cases to ordinary deterministic history does not introduce a difference that restores responsibility. Critics dispute whether the cases are specified well enough to establish that claim.

## 2. The Central Question

As the story moves from direct intervention to an ordinary deterministic history, does any change make Plum responsible—or do the cases preserve everything relevant to the verdict?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument challenges compatibilism, the view that moral responsibility can coexist with determinism. Its reasoning is conditional: if Plum is not responsible in the manipulated cases, and if no responsibility-relevant difference appears as the story moves to Case 4, then determinism undermines the control required for the targeted kind of responsibility. The [archived SEP supplement](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/compatibilism/supplement.html) lays out this progression.

The first case does not elicit a universal verdict. The current [SEP discussion](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-arguments/) says the first three Plums are under-described: how their details are filled in can change whether readers see a relevant difference from ordinary deterministic agents.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Hard-line compatibilists** deny that the specified manipulation defeats responsibility, or argue that Plum should receive the same responsibility verdict as an ordinary deterministic agent if there is no relevant difference. **Soft-line compatibilists** accept that the manipulation cases undermine responsibility but argue that their causal histories differ in a morally relevant way from ordinary upbringing and development. The [SEP discussion](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-arguments/) and [Jeppsson’s analysis](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1303683/FULLTEXT03) describe these competing replies; neither is presented here as settled.

The target is **basic-desert responsibility**: deserving praise or blame simply because of what one did and its moral status. That does not by itself settle whether protective, rehabilitative, or other forward-looking responses to wrongdoing are justified ([Caruso, 2014](https://researcherslinks.com/current-issues/Precis-of-Derk-Pereboom-Free-Will-Agency-and-Meaning-in-Life/9/13/51/html)). Incompatibilists may use Pereboom’s argument, but it is too broad to say that all incompatibilists accept this particular argument.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument does not assume that determinism is true; Case 4 asks what follows if it is. It is also not a demonstration that the four cases are equivalent. The inference depends on the judgments about the manipulated cases and on the claim that no responsibility-relevant difference blocks the move to Case 4.

Pereboom later revised Case 1 after objections to the original intervention. Jeppsson (2020) describes the later version as significantly weaker and notes that it may make the intended intuition less forceful. This entry summarizes the 2001 sequence and does not treat either version as decisive.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is Plum responsible in Case 1? What feature of the setup drives your answer?
2. Which transition to Case 4, if any, introduces a responsibility-relevant difference?
3. Should a history of manipulation matter if an agent can still recognize and respond to reasons?
4. How much weight should an argument place on intuitions about hypothetical cases?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Other manipulation arguments use different setups, including Mele’s Ernie/zygote case discussed in the [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incompatibilism-arguments/). These arguments share a family resemblance but are not simply Pereboom’s four cases with different names; their causal histories and replies can differ.
