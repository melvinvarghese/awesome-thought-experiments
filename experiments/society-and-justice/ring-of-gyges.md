# The Ring of Gyges

**Category:** society-and-justice  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Plato, Glaucon (dialogue speaker)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Republic, Book II, fourth century BCE  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** justice, motivation, impunity  
**Related experiments:** [The State of Nature](state-of-nature.md); [The Veil of Ignorance and Original Position](veil-of-ignorance.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In Plato’s Republic, Glaucon tells of a ring that makes its wearer invisible. Its possessor uses the power to gain access, commit grave wrongs, and acquire political power. Glaucon then imagines giving such power to a person regarded as just and another regarded as unjust. Would both use their freedom from detection to pursue advantage?

The ring is a device for removing ordinary consequences of being seen. The challenge is not simply whether invisibility would be exciting. It asks whether people value justice for itself or chiefly because punishment and reputation make injustice costly.

## 2. The Central Question

Would justice remain worth choosing if a person could act unjustly without being caught or losing a good reputation?

## 3. Why It Matters

The story separates moral motivation from prudential incentives. It also distinguishes what people would do from what they have reason to do. Even if many would misuse the ring, that would not by itself establish that misuse is justified or that a just life lacks value.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Glaucon’s challenge:** ordinary conformity to justice may reflect vulnerability and fear rather than intrinsic commitment. The ring strengthens the demand for an account of why justice benefits its possessor.

**Platonic response:** the Republic develops an account connecting justice with the organization and health of the soul. The argument seeks a value deeper than social applause, though assessing it requires more than the ring story alone.

**Moral-reasons response:** one can also question whether every reason for justice must be reduced to personal gain. That challenges the framing of the demand instead of merely predicting better behavior from an invisible person.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The provocative challenge is spoken by Glaucon within a dialogue; it is not simply Plato’s final endorsement of injustice. The hypothetical power also does not make consequences disappear for victims. It removes an important class of costs to the agent, which makes the distinction between self-interest and moral concern sharper.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which reasons to refrain from harm remain when detection is impossible?
2. Would someone who acts justly only from fear count as a just person?
3. Does showing that injustice damages the wrongdoer give the right kind of moral reason?
4. What evidence about actual behavior could inform the discussion without settling the normative question?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Glaucon’s comparison of apparent and actual justice extends the challenge by contrasting reputation with character. Modern anonymity can provide a partial analogy, but it rarely supplies the complete impunity stipulated by the ring. Anonymous actions still have institutional and interpersonal consequences.
