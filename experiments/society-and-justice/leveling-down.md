# The Leveling-Down Objection

**Category:** society-and-justice  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Derek Parfit, Larry Temkin (prominent debate)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Contemporary equality debate; Parfit’s “Equality and Priority” (1997)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** equality, priority, welfare  
**Related experiments:** [The Veil of Ignorance and Original Position](veil-of-ignorance.md); [Wilt Chamberlain’s Earnings](wilt-chamberlain.md); [Dworkin’s Resource Auction](dworkins-auction.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Imagine two groups with unequal levels of well-being. A proposed change reduces the better-off group to the worse-off group’s level and provides no benefit to anybody. No resources are transferred; no domination is removed; no future advantage results. The outcome is more equal, but some people are worse off and nobody is better off.

The stipulation excluding indirect benefits matters. Real policies that reduce an advantage may improve fair competition, political standing, or public services. Those policies do not automatically satisfy the pure case’s premise that no one benefits.

## 2. The Central Question

Is a more equal outcome better in any respect when the only change is making some people worse off?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case tests whether equality has independent value or whether our concern is better explained by helping people who fare badly. It also separates “better overall” from “better in one respect.” An objection to one judgment need not establish the other, and moral pluralists can assign more than one value to a situation.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Prioritarian response:** a benefit matters more when it goes to someone worse off, but harming the better-off helps no one. Priority can explain concern for disadvantage without treating the pure reduction as an improvement.

**Pluralist egalitarian response:** equality might improve in one respect while the outcome becomes worse overall because losses outweigh that improvement. Critics question whether the purported improvement is intelligible without benefiting anyone.

**Relational response:** the central ideal may concern equal standing rather than identical welfare levels. Removing oppressive power can matter, but that is not the benefit-free reduction stipulated in the pure objection.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument does not refute every egalitarian theory. Equality of status, fair opportunity, and reduction of poverty are different aims. Nor should a tax-funded service be called leveling down simply because someone pays more: recipients may benefit. The thought experiment deliberately removes such effects to isolate a value claim.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Can a situation improve in one respect while becoming worse overall?
2. Is equality between persons a value that must benefit a particular person?
3. Would your verdict change if the inequality involved political power rather than welfare?
4. What empirical information would be necessary before applying this objection to a real policy?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Examples using lost sight or destroyed resources sharpen the same structure, but disability should not be treated as a complete measure of a person’s well-being. Prioritarian and relational theories offer distinct alternatives. They should be compared on their own commitments rather than grouped as a single reply.
