# Wilt Chamberlain’s Earnings

**Category:** society-and-justice  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Robert Nozick  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** liberty, property, distribution  
**Related experiments:** [The Veil of Ignorance and Original Position](veil-of-ignorance.md); [Dworkin’s Resource Auction](dworkins-auction.md); [The Leveling-Down Objection](leveling-down.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Begin with a distribution of resources that you accept as just. A popular basketball player, Wilt Chamberlain, arranges to receive an extra payment from each spectator who voluntarily chooses to watch him play. Many people agree. At the end, Chamberlain has become much wealthier than others, although each spectator willingly paid from resources they were entitled to spend.

Nozick uses the case to ask whether a just starting point plus acceptable transfers can lead to an unjust result merely because the resulting pattern differs. The real athlete supplies a recognizable name; the distribution and payment arrangement are hypothetical.

## 2. The Central Question

Can voluntary exchanges upset a just distribution, and what would justify intervention to restore a preferred pattern?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case contrasts historical approaches to justice with approaches that evaluate patterns of holdings. It makes freedom to use resources part of the distributive question. It also prompts scrutiny of background rules: which rights did the original distribution confer, and what obligations accompany transactions?

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Entitlement response:** if people legitimately hold their resources and voluntarily transfer them, Chamberlain’s new holdings are legitimate. Repeatedly restoring a fixed pattern restricts those freedoms.

**Institutional response:** an entitlement to spend resources can coexist with taxes or shared obligations already built into a just system. A transfer need not confer an unlimited right immune from those rules.

**Background-justice response:** real holdings may reflect coercion, exclusion, or previous injustice. The thought experiment stipulates an acceptable starting distribution, so it cannot establish that actual wealth is automatically deserved. Nozick’s broader theory itself includes rectification.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The case is not a historical financial claim about Chamberlain. Nor does it show that every voluntary exchange preserves justice regardless of initial rights or effects on outsiders. Equally, an egalitarian response need not forbid every gift or require identical outcomes after each transaction. The precise distributive principle matters.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does a just allocation give an unlimited right to transfer, or rights defined by institutional rules?
2. Would a tax known in advance invalidate the spectators’ freedom to choose?
3. Could individually acceptable transactions cumulatively alter others’ opportunities or political power?
4. What work is done by stipulating that the initial distribution is just?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Gifts, inheritances, and payments to artists illustrate the same challenge, while differing in morally relevant details. The original argument addresses more than strict equality: any continually maintained pattern must explain how it accommodates free transfers.
