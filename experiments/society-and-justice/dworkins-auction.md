# Dworkin’s Resource Auction

**Category:** society-and-justice  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Ronald Dworkin  
**Original publication or approximate period:** “What Is Equality? Part 2: Equality of Resources” (1981)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** resources, equality, responsibility  
**Related experiments:** [The Veil of Ignorance and Original Position](veil-of-ignorance.md); [Wilt Chamberlain’s Earnings](wilt-chamberlain.md); [The Leveling-Down Objection](leveling-down.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Imagine people dividing initially unowned resources. Give each person the same purchasing power and let them bid for different bundles. They have different projects, so an equal distribution need not give everyone identical things. The proposed outcome must satisfy an envy test: no one would prefer another person’s bundle of resources to their own.

The auction treats the cost of a bundle partly through what others would give up to obtain it. The idealization begins a larger account of resource equality, including difficult questions about personal capacities, luck, and responsibility. It is not a claim that one auction solves all injustice.

## 2. The Central Question

Can equal starting purchasing power and an envy-free allocation represent fair resource shares while respecting diverse ambitions?

## 3. Why It Matters

The device distinguishes equality of resources from equality of welfare and from identical possessions. It makes opportunity cost relevant: using a scarce resource for one plan prevents its use for another. It also forces a question about what people can fairly be held responsible for when plans succeed or fail.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Resource-egalitarian response:** equal bidding power respects different preferences while making the costs of resource choices visible. Different bundles can be equally fair without producing identical satisfaction.

**Luck-sensitive criticism:** equal external resources may leave people unequally able to use them because of circumstances they did not choose. Dworkin’s broader account introduces further devices, including hypothetical insurance, rather than ignoring this difficulty.

**Institutional criticism:** an envy test and ideal auction require information and assumptions about preferences, capacities, and market conditions. A critic can accept equal concern while doubting that this mechanism captures all of its requirements.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The envy test is a comparison of bundles, not a recommendation to cultivate jealousy or inspect emotions. Equal money in an actual unequal society is also not the ideal case’s equal starting position. Later unequal results cannot be assessed responsibly without asking which risks were chosen and which disadvantages were imposed.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Can two very different bundles count as equal shares without a common welfare measure?
2. How should scarce resources be priced when preferences conflict?
3. Which personal circumstances should the auction hold fixed, and which need compensation?
4. Could an allocation pass an envy test and still preserve unequal social power?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Hypothetical insurance extends the account to risks and personal circumstances. Comparing the auction with Rawls’s original position highlights different uses of idealization: one models resource allocation under equal means, the other selection of principles without personal bargaining information.
