# The Turing Test

**Category:** artificial-intelligence  
**Associated thinker(s):** Alan M. Turing  
**Original publication or approximate period:** “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Type:** model  
**Themes:** intelligence; behavior; evidence  
**Related experiments:** paperclip-maximizer; ai-box

## 1. The Scenario

An interrogator exchanges written messages with two unseen respondents, one human and one machine. The task is to identify which is which. The machine tries to participate convincingly enough that its identity cannot reliably be distinguished under the chosen conditions.

Turing’s original discussion begins with a human imitation game and develops a machine version. Modern summaries differ over details. Any actual evaluation must specify interview length, participants, instructions, comparison population, scoring, and access to other tools. A vague report that someone was fooled once does not establish success under every version.

## 2. The Central Question

What evidential weight should competent conversational performance carry when judging whether a machine can think?

## 3. Why It Matters

The imitation game shifts discussion from an undefined verbal dispute to observable interaction. Its strength is that behavior can be compared publicly. Its limitation is that selecting a behavioral task does not automatically establish what capacities the task measures, whether the measure generalizes, or whether it reaches consciousness.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**An evidential reading** treats sustained, flexible linguistic performance as significant evidence for intelligence, analogous in some respects to evidence used for other minds. **A criterion reading** makes performance more directly constitutive of the tested status. **Critics** argue that imitation may reward deception, depend too much on human limitations, or miss intelligent systems whose abilities are unlike ours. A critic must still explain which alternative evidence would be better and why.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The test does not directly measure benevolence, truthfulness, consciousness, or safety. Turing did not define every later chatbot competition. Passing a short, narrow interaction is different from demonstrating robust competence across contexts. Conversely, failing to resemble a human conversational partner does not logically establish the absence of every kind of intelligence.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What interview would distinguish memorized responses from flexible understanding?
2. Should an excellent nonhuman scientist have to imitate a human’s conversational quirks?
3. Would evidence of intelligence be sufficient evidence of moral status or safe behavior?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Embodied or “total” variants add perception and action. The Chinese room challenges the inference from successful symbol manipulation to understanding; the paperclip scenario asks what an intelligent agent values.
