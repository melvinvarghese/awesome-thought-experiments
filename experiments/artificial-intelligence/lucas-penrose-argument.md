# The Lucas–Penrose Argument

**Category:** artificial-intelligence  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** J. R. Lucas, Roger Penrose  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Lucas, “Minds, Machines and Gödel” (Philosophy, 1961); Penrose, The Emperor’s New Mind (1989)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** Gödel, mechanism, mathematical insight  
**Related experiments:** [Chinese Room](../consciousness/chinese-room.md); [Russell’s Paradox](../logic/russells-paradox.md); [The Liar Paradox](../logic/liar.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem says that for any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic, there is a sentence that the system cannot prove but that is true, if the system is consistent. Lucas argued that if the mind were a machine, it would be equivalent to such a system.

For any such machine, a human mathematician can construct its Gödel sentence and see that it is true, which the machine cannot prove. Therefore, the argument concludes, the mind is not a machine.

## 2. The Central Question

Does Gödel’s theorem show that human mathematical understanding cannot be captured by any formal system or computer program?

## 3. Why It Matters

If the argument worked, it would settle a major question about artificial intelligence and refute mechanism. It has been criticized for reasons that illustrate what is needed in any argument from mathematics to the mind.

The case also shows how a theorem in logic can be misapplied. Careful statements of what Gödel proved do not by themselves license claims about human minds.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Critics** argue that the mathematician cannot be sure that the system is consistent, and that the argument assumes this. **Others** note that the human mind may be inconsistent or may not be a single fixed system. **Penrose’s version** adds a claim that physical processes in the brain are non-computable. **Defenders** hold that a version of the argument survives these objections, which many logicians deny.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Gödel’s theorem itself is a mathematical result and is not in doubt. The argument that it shows minds are not machines is a separate philosophical claim, and most logicians and philosophers regard it as unsuccessful. It does not show that humans can see the truth of every mathematical statement.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What does the mathematician need to know about the machine to construct its Gödel sentence?
2. Could the human mind be inconsistent?
3. Does the argument prove too much, for example about any human working with a fixed procedure?
4. How is this different from the Chinese Room?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Penrose’s later work combines the argument with a proposal about quantum processes in the brain. That proposal is empirical and is evaluated on different grounds.
