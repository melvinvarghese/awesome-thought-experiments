# The Lucas–Penrose Argument

**Category:** artificial-intelligence  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** J. R. Lucas, Roger Penrose  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Lucas, “Minds, Machines and Gödel” (Philosophy, 1961); Penrose, The Emperor’s New Mind (1989)\
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** Gödel, mechanism, mathematical insight  
**Related experiments:** [Chinese Room](../consciousness/chinese-room.md); [Russell’s Paradox](../logic/russells-paradox.md); [The Liar Paradox](../logic/liar.md)

## 1. The Scenario

For a consistent, effectively axiomatized formal theory T that contains enough arithmetic, Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem gives a sentence Gₜ that T cannot prove. With the standard construction, Gₜ is true in the standard natural numbers if T is consistent. Lucas argued that a machine model of mathematical reasoning would be subject to such a limit, while a human who could justifiably recognize the model’s consistency could see its Gödel sentence as true.

Critics question whether a human can establish that consistency for a proposed model and whether human mathematical reasoning can be represented by one fixed formal system.

## 2. The Central Question

Does Gödel’s theorem show that human mathematical understanding cannot be captured by any formal system or computer program?

## 3. Why It Matters

If successful, the argument would challenge mechanism and computational accounts of mathematical reasoning. The theorem concerns formal systems; applying it to minds requires additional claims about how a machine models reasoning and what a human can know about that model. Those philosophical claims are disputed.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Lucas argues from a candidate machine’s Gödel sentence to a limit on mechanism. A standard objection is that recognizing the sentence as true requires a justified consistency or soundness judgment about that particular system. Lucas acknowledged objections about human inconsistency and whether reasoning fits one fixed system, and offered replies. Critics including Putnam, Boolos, Davis, Feferman, and Shapiro challenge different assumptions and steps; this is a selected set of positions, not a count of scholarly opinion.

Penrose later extends the non-computability argument into a proposed new physics of mind. In *Shadows of the Mind*, he discusses quantum theory and the brain as part of that proposal. These further hypotheses are distinct from Gödel’s theorem and require separate scientific assessment.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The theorem concerns a particular formal system and its own Gödel sentence. Even if a version of the argument worked for a candidate system, it would not show that humans can recognize the truth of every mathematical statement. The anti-mechanist conclusion depends on additional assumptions about human reasoning.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What does the mathematician need to know about the machine to construct its Gödel sentence?
2. Could the human mind be inconsistent?
3. Does the argument prove too much, for example about any human working with a fixed procedure?
4. How is this different from the Chinese Room?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Penrose’s later work links his non-computability thesis to a proposed new physics of mind, including discussion of quantum theory and the brain. These further hypotheses are not established by Gödel’s theorem.
