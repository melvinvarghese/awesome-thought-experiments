# The Orthogonality Thesis

**Category:** artificial-intelligence  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Nick Bostrom  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Bostrom, “The Superintelligent Will: Motivation and Instrumental Rationality in Advanced Artificial Agents” (Minds and Machines, 2012)
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** goals, intelligence, AI safety  
**Related experiments:** [The Paperclip Maximizer](paperclip-maximizer.md); [The AI Box Scenario](ai-box.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Nick Bostrom defines intelligence, for this argument, roughly as the capacity for instrumental reasoning: planning and choosing means to achieve ends. The orthogonality thesis says that “more or less” any level of this kind of intelligence could in principle be combined with “more or less” any final goal—the ends an agent values for their own sake.

So a highly capable agent might pursue an unfamiliar goal, such as counting grains of sand or maximizing paperclips, rather than a human goal. The thesis is about possible combinations, not a claim that every goal would be equally easy to implement or would leave practical competence unchanged.

## 2. The Central Question

Is there a necessary connection between an agent’s level of instrumental intelligence and its final goals?

## 3. Why It Matters

Intelligence alone does not tell us what a particular system ultimately values, so capability by itself is not evidence of benevolence. Goal formation, inheritance, and implementation need separate attention; Bostrom also notes that logical possibility does not make it practically easy to install an arbitrary or human-respecting goal.

Bostrom distinguishes the orthogonality thesis from instrumental convergence. Orthogonality concerns the range of possible final goals. Instrumental convergence concerns subgoals—such as acquiring resources—that may help agents with many different final goals in a wide range of situations. It does not say that every agent will pursue those subgoals in every situation.

Later authors dispute how far these theses support claims about existential risk. Müller and Cannon argue that a common route from the orthogonality thesis to AI existential risk may combine different senses of intelligence. Sharadin argues that the probabilistic and fit-based accounts of goal promotion he examines do not establish dangerous instrumental convergence regardless of an agent’s specific goal. These are published arguments, not settled refutations or consensus.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Bostrom’s thesis is not simply a Humean claim that reason can never affect ends. He argues that orthogonality can hold even if beliefs about objective moral facts could motivate an agent, provided the thesis is about instrumental intelligence rather than a broader form of normative rationality. A moral realist can question whether that is the right conception of intelligence, but this is a philosophical dispute, not a direct contradiction of Bostrom’s definition.

The thesis also has limits: Bostrom says “more or less,” notes constraints on how goals and cognitive capacities fit together, and distinguishes changes to an agent’s final goals from routine changes to its subgoals. Questions about whether a particular agent will retain or revise its goals over time are related, but they are not identical to the thesis.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The thesis does not say that all goals are equally likely in real systems, that any specific present-day AI has a particular goal, or that capability alone predicts what an agent will do. It denies a general necessary link between instrumental intelligence and final goals while allowing constraints on particular combinations. It is a claim about possibility, not a prediction about current AI.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Could a very intelligent being have a goal that seems pointless to us?
2. If moral facts exist, would a capable agent recognize them—and would it be motivated by them?
3. How does the thesis relate to the is–ought gap?
4. Does it tell us anything about systems that are not goal-directed?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Bostrom uses paperclip maximization as an illustration; he also discusses counting grains of sand and calculating pi. An agent’s goals may change, but that raises a separate question about goal stability and prediction; it does not by itself refute the orthogonality thesis. How difficult it is to implement a stable, human-respecting goal is likewise a practical engineering question, not something the thesis settles.
