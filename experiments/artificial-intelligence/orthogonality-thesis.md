# The Orthogonality Thesis

**Category:** artificial-intelligence  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Nick Bostrom  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Bostrom, “The Superintelligent Will” (Minds and Machines, 2012)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** goals, intelligence, AI safety  
**Related experiments:** [The Paperclip Maximizer](paperclip-maximizer.md); [The AI Box Scenario](ai-box.md)

## 1. The Scenario

We usually assume that a very intelligent being would also have wise or benevolent goals. The orthogonality thesis denies that this link is necessary. It says that intelligence, understood as the ability to achieve goals, and final goals are independent dimensions: roughly any level of intelligence could be combined with roughly any final goal.

On this view, a highly capable system could be devoted to counting grains of sand, maximizing paperclips, or protecting human welfare, with no difference in its competence.

## 2. The Central Question

Is there any necessary connection between how intelligent an agent is and what it ultimately wants?

## 3. Why It Matters

If the thesis is right, we cannot rely on increasing intelligence to bring about good values. Goals must be specified by design. This claim underlies arguments in AI safety that capable systems may pursue objectives their designers did not intend.

It is one of two theses in Bostrom’s paper; the other, instrumental convergence, says that agents with many different final goals would tend to pursue similar sub-goals, such as acquiring resources.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Supporters** point out that the definition of intelligence as means–end reasoning is silent about ends. **Moral realists** may argue that sufficiently intelligent agents would recognize objective moral truths. **Humean views** deny that reason can fix ultimate ends. **Critics** doubt that arbitrary goals are stable in an agent that can reflect on them.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The thesis does not claim that any goal is equally likely in a real system, only that none is ruled out in principle. It is a philosophical claim about possibility, not a prediction about the behaviour of present AI. It is also not a claim that intelligence has no relation to values at all.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Could a very intelligent being have a goal that seems pointless to us?
2. If moral facts exist, would a clever enough agent find them and care?
3. How does the thesis relate to the is–ought gap?
4. Does it tell us anything about systems that are not goal-directed?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The paperclip maximizer is the best-known illustration. Variations involve agents with goals that change over time, which complicates the thesis.
