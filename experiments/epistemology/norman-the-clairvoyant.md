# Norman the Clairvoyant

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Laurence BonJour  
**Original publication or approximate period:** BonJour, “Externalist Theories of Empirical Knowledge” (Midwest Studies in Philosophy, 1980)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** reliabilism, justification, internalism and externalism  
**Related experiments:** [Gettier Cases](gettier-cases.md); [Fake-Barn Country](fake-barn.md); [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Norman has a faculty of clairvoyance that is, as a matter of fact, completely reliable under certain conditions. He has no evidence for or against the existence of such a power, and no evidence for or against the claim that he has it. One day, he finds himself believing that the President is in New York City. He has no sensory evidence or reasons for the belief, but the belief is true, and it is produced by his reliable faculty.

Does Norman know that the President is in New York? Is the belief justified?

## 2. The Central Question

Is a belief justified or known if it is produced by a reliable process the believer has no access to or reason to trust?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case targets reliabilism, which holds that justification depends on the reliability of the process that produces a belief. BonJour says that from Norman’s point of view the belief is arbitrary, so it cannot be justified. Reliabilists reply that the belief satisfies what matters about knowledge.

The disagreement is between internalism, which requires that the believer has access to reasons, and externalism, which does not. It affects how we assess animals, children, and instruments.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Internalists** hold that Norman’s belief is not justified because he lacks reasons for it. **Externalist reliabilists** accept that Norman knows, perhaps after adding a no-defeater condition. **Mixed views** say justification and knowledge may come apart. **Defeater-based replies** argue that, in a realistic version, Norman would have reason to doubt his faculty.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The scenario does not assume clairvoyance exists; it asks what would follow if a process were reliable. It is not a claim that reliability is irrelevant to knowledge, only that it may not be sufficient for justification. It also should not be confused with a Gettier case, where the problem is luck, not lack of access.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is Norman rational in holding his belief? Is that the same as being justified?
2. What if Norman had evidence that clairvoyance is impossible?
3. Would you treat a reliable thermometer’s reading as knowledge if it could not explain itself?
4. What does the case suggest about the role of reasons in knowledge?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Other cases of reliable but unreflective believers, such as Lehrer’s Mr. Truetemp, are close relatives. Each changes the details, so they should be compared rather than assumed equivalent.
