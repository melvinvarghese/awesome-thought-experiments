# Descartes’s Evil Demon

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** René Descartes  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), Meditation I  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** skepticism, certainty, methodological doubt  
**Related experiments:** [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md); [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Set aside, for an exercise, your usual trust in perception. Suppose an extraordinarily capable deceiver arranges your apparent experiences and misleads your thinking. Your room, body, and even very familiar judgments may be less secure than they appear. The challenge is to identify a belief whose truth does not depend on the deceiver’s good behavior.

Descartes introduces this hypothesis as a device for sustaining doubt, not as a report that a malicious being exists. Its force comes from how thoroughly it removes ordinary assurances. The later search for a secure starting point belongs to the larger argument of the Meditations.

## 2. The Central Question

Is there anything you can know even while supposing that a powerful deceiver is misleading you?

## 3. Why It Matters

Ordinary error correction usually appeals to another perception, calculation, or trusted witness. Radical deception questions the reliability of those resources together. This makes the case a test of foundations: must knowledge start with something immune to all possible doubt, or is that demand itself too strong?

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Foundational reading:** the exercise clears away uncertain commitments so that inquiry can begin from a secure point. The fact that thinking occurs becomes central to Descartes’s subsequent argument.

**Skeptical reading:** if apparently excellent evidence is compatible with deception, recovering extensive knowledge may be much harder than showing that some thinking exists.

**Fallibilist criticism:** knowledge ordinarily tolerates possible error. Requiring invulnerability to an extravagant hypothesis may replace the everyday concept with an unusually demanding ideal. These approaches disagree about the appropriate standard, not merely about how frightening the story is.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The deceiver is not Descartes’s final account of reality. The claim that thinking occurs does not immediately establish an enduring immaterial person, a body, or a trustworthy external world. Nor should the dream argument and demon hypothesis be treated as interchangeable: the latter deliberately extends the scope of doubt.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which belief could a deceiver make false simply by changing your experiences?
2. Does the occurrence of doubt establish more than a present mental event?
3. What distinguishes a reason to investigate reliability from a reason to abandon every belief?
4. Could a community of mutually checking investigators answer a hypothesis that deceives the whole community?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

A computer simulation replaces the intentional supernatural deceiver with technology. Brain-in-a-vat cases add questions about reference and environmental history. Neither adaptation automatically inherits all assumptions of Descartes’s original method.
