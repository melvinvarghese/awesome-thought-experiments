# Hume’s Missing Shade of Blue

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** David Hume  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) and An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** empiricism, ideas and impressions, imagination  
**Related experiments:** [Mary’s Room: The Knowledge Argument](../consciousness/marys-room.md); [Molyneux’s Problem](molyneux-problem.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Hume holds that all our simple ideas, such as the idea of a colour, are copies of earlier simple impressions, which come from experience. He then considers a possible counterexample. Suppose a person has seen every shade of blue except one, and that these shades are laid out in order from darkest to lightest, with a gap where the missing shade belongs.

Hume suggests that such a person could notice the gap and might be able to supply the idea of the missing shade from imagination without ever having seen it.

## 2. The Central Question

Can we form a genuinely new simple idea without a matching experience, and what would that show about empiricism?

## 3. Why It Matters

The principle that ideas derive from experience is central to empiricism. If it fails even once, the claim that all content comes from sense experience loses its absolute character. Hume himself treats the case as a very singular exception, not worth altering the general rule.

The scenario is also an early example of reasoning about what imagination can do with materials from experience, which matters for modern debates about what Mary learns in the knowledge argument.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Hume’s own treatment** is to concede the exception but regard it as too minor to matter. **Critics of empiricism** argue that if one can do this for a shade, the principle is not a strict law. **Defenders** say that the missing shade is composed from similar ideas and therefore does not need a new simple impression. **Contemporary readers** note links to questions about phenomenal concepts and whether experience is required for understanding.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Hume does not say that the exception refutes his view, and the case is not a general proof that knowledge can come without experience. It also does not show that people can imagine colours unlike any they have seen; the claim is about filling a gap between known shades.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Could you imagine a shade you have never seen? What would that show?
2. Is the missing shade different in kind from a colour entirely outside your experience?
3. Why might Hume think one exception is not enough to alter his principle?
4. How does this compare with what Mary seems to learn on leaving her room?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Extensions ask about sounds, tastes, or entire sensory modalities, where there may be no neighbouring ideas to interpolate between. Those cases are stronger tests of the copy principle.
