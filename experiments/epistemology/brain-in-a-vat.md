# Brain in a Vat

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Hilary Putnam  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Reason, Truth and History (1981), chapter 1; earlier skeptical precursors  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** skepticism, meaning, externalism  
**Related experiments:** [Descartes’s Evil Demon](evil-demon.md); [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Imagine a brain kept alive in a laboratory. A computer supplies the inputs that normally arrive through a body, producing an apparently ordinary life: friends, rain, books, and the laboratory itself. In the stipulated case, inspecting your surroundings supplies no simple test: the simulated inspection is part of the story.

There are importantly different versions. A person might have been recently placed in the vat, retaining ordinary linguistic connections. Alternatively, a brain might have existed only within a simulation. Putnam’s argument about reference targets a carefully specified version of the latter case; it is not a universal laboratory detector.

## 2. The Central Question

If your experiences could occur in a radically deceptive environment, what entitles you to say that you know you have a body?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case separates experience from the world it seems to reveal. It also connects epistemology to the theory of meaning. If what a word refers to partly depends on a speaker’s environment and history, changing the environment may change the thought itself, even when the subject notices no difference.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Skeptical response:** experiential evidence seems compatible with ordinary life and radical deception. If knowing requires excluding the deceptive alternative, ordinary knowledge is threatened.

**Semantic externalist response:** a lifelong vat inhabitant may not refer to real brains or vats with those words. Putnam uses this complication to resist a particular skeptical self-description. Critics ask whether the response establishes the bodily knowledge we wanted, or only exposes a limitation in one formulation.

**Fallibilist response:** knowing may not require ruling out every imaginable possibility. This approach changes the standard of knowledge rather than locating a hidden sensory test.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Conceivability does not establish that the technology is physically possible or that anyone is actually envatted. Nor does a semantic objection establish that every skeptical alternative is false. Keep recently envatted and lifelong-envatted cases separate: a reply to one need not answer the other.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What would count as evidence against the hypothesis if every apparent test is simulated?
2. Does inability to exclude a possibility make it reasonable to believe it?
3. Would a lifelong simulated person’s word “tree” refer to trees, computer patterns, or neither?
4. Which ordinary knowledge claim would you most want an anti-skeptical argument to recover?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Descartes’s deceiver is a historical precursor. Contemporary virtual-world scenarios alter questions of embodiment, causal contact, and whether simulated objects are themselves real. These changes should be stated, because a functioning virtual world need not be deceptive in every respect.
