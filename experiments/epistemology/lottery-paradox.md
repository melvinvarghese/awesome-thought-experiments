# The Lottery Paradox

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Henry Kyburg  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Kyburg, Probability and the Logic of Rational Belief (1961)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** rational belief, probability, knowledge  
**Related experiments:** [Gettier Cases](gettier-cases.md); [Sleeping Beauty](../mathematics/sleeping-beauty.md)

## 1. The Scenario

A fair lottery has a million tickets and exactly one will win. For any particular ticket, it is extremely likely to lose, so it seems rational to believe that this ticket will lose. The same reasoning applies to every ticket. If rational belief is closed under conjunction, you should then believe that all the tickets will lose.

But you also know that one ticket will win. You are led to believe a contradiction, each step of which seemed reasonable.

## 2. The Central Question

Can it be rational to believe each of a set of claims that you know cannot all be true, and what should we say about the relation between high probability and belief?

## 3. Why It Matters

The paradox shows a conflict between two plausible principles: that high probability suffices for rational belief, and that rational belief is closed under conjunction. It forces a decision about whether belief is a matter of degree or an all-or-nothing state.

It also bears on knowledge. Most people feel that you do not know your ticket will lose, even if your evidence is overwhelming, which suggests that knowledge requires more than high probability.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Rejecting conjunction closure** allows rational belief in each claim but not their conjunction. **Raising the threshold to certainty** denies belief in each losing claim. **Credence-first views** say that only degrees of confidence matter. **Knowledge-first views** link the puzzle to why we seem not to know lottery propositions. A related preface paradox applies similar reasoning to an author who believes each claim in a book but also that the book contains an error.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox is not about the arithmetic of lotteries, and it does not depend on a specific number of tickets. It is a puzzle about how belief relates to probability. Nor does it show that probabilistic reasoning is unreliable.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Do you believe your ticket will lose? Do you know it?
2. Why might people feel differently about a lottery claim and a claim based on testimony, with the same probability?
3. Is the preface paradox the same problem?
4. How would you describe the difference between belief and confidence?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Increasing the number of tickets makes each loss claim more probable but does not remove the paradox. The preface paradox, attributed to David Makinson, runs a similar structure without a lottery.
