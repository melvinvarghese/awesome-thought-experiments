# Hempel’s Paradox of the Ravens

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Carl Hempel  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Hempel, “Studies in the Logic of Confirmation” (Mind, 1945)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** confirmation, induction, evidence  
**Related experiments:** [Goodman’s New Riddle of Induction (Grue)](grue.md); [The Monty Hall Problem](../mathematics/monty-hall.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Consider the hypothesis “All ravens are black.” A black raven seems to support it. Now note that this hypothesis is logically equivalent to “Everything that is not black is not a raven.” A white shoe is not black and is not a raven, so it seems to support the second statement. Assuming that evidence for a statement is evidence for anything equivalent to it, the white shoe supports the hypothesis about ravens.

That seems absurd. A tidy indoor survey of shoes and teacups would seem to tell us something about ornithology.

## 2. The Central Question

Why does it seem wrong that a white shoe confirms a claim about ravens, and which assumption should we revise?

## 3. Why It Matters

The paradox arises from three plausible principles about confirmation: that instances confirm generalizations, that confirmation is closed under logical equivalence, and that confirmation depends only on logical form. Together they yield an implausible result. It is a central test case for any theory of evidence.

It also shows that our judgments about relevance depend on background beliefs, such as how many ravens and how many non-black things there are.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Hempel’s own response** accepts the conclusion but says it seems odd because of background knowledge; the confirmation is real and negligible. **Bayesian analyses** show that the evidence from a white shoe is real but tiny, since non-black things are extremely numerous. **Revisionist approaches** drop one of the three principles. **Good-style examples** suggest that, in some background circumstances, finding a black raven could even disconfirm the hypothesis.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox does not show that shoes tell us anything useful about birds in practice, and it is not a logical contradiction. It reveals a tension among principles that look innocent. The paradox is also not resolved simply by saying it is unscientific to survey shoes.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which of the three principles would you give up, and what would you lose?
2. Does it matter how the white object was selected? Would sampling non-black things at random differ from sampling ravens?
3. How does background knowledge change how much a piece of evidence supports a claim?
4. Can you think of a case where a black raven would count against the hypothesis?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions with other generalizations, such as “All emeralds are green,” lead naturally to Goodman’s riddle about which generalizations evidence supports.
