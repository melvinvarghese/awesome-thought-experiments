# Molyneux’s Problem

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** William Molyneux, John Locke  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Letters to Locke (1688 and 1693); Locke’s Essay, second edition (1694)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** perception, cross-modal recognition, learning  
**Related experiments:** [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md); [Gettier Cases](gettier-cases.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Imagine a person blind from birth who can reliably distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch. Suppose that the person acquires vision and sees the two objects on a table, without touching them. Could the person immediately identify which visible object is the cube and which is the sphere?

The word “immediately” is crucial: recognizing them after practice would not answer the same question. So is the restriction on touch during the test. Molyneux first sent a version to Locke in 1688 and returned to it in 1693; Locke published the question in the 1694 edition of his Essay.

## 2. The Central Question

Does knowledge of shape transfer directly between touch and sight, or must the connection between these senses be learned?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case links philosophical accounts of ideas with questions about perceptual learning. It can be discussed imaginatively, but it also motivates empirical investigation when suitable clinical circumstances exist. Those investigations must consider the quality of restored vision, previous experience, instructions, and the difference between early performance and later learning.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Experience-based response:** successful touch–vision matching requires learned associations between sensory presentations. Familiarity with shape through touch alone does not guarantee immediate visual identification.

**Shared-structure response:** both senses may represent spatial organization in ways that permit some transfer. A defender must explain whether that capacity is innate, conceptual, or acquired through other activities.

**Empirical response:** studies of newly sighted participants can constrain predictions, although their results concern particular medical histories and tasks. A negative initial result need not establish that touch and vision have no shared representation whatsoever.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The thought experiment does not ask whether a blind person can understand geometry. It concerns a specific recognition task after a change in sensory access. It also should not be used to portray blindness as absence of intelligence or spatial understanding. The philosophical “instant restoration” idealization differs from clinical recovery.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would naming the shapes differ from matching one touched shape to a visible object?
2. What prior experience should be held constant for a fair test?
3. Could rapid learning support a shared structure even if immediate matching fails?
4. Which result would count against your preferred explanation rather than merely require a small adjustment?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Later studies and philosophical variations examine cross-modal matching, distance judgments, and the role of movement. The original correspondence also considered reaching. These extensions can illuminate different capacities, but they should not be treated as interchangeable measurements of a single all-purpose ability to see.
