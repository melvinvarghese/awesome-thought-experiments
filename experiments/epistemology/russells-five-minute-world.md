# Russell’s Five-Minute World

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Bertrand Russell  
**Original publication or approximate period:** The Analysis of Mind (1921), Lecture IX: Memory  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** memory, past, skepticism  
**Related experiments:** [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md); [Gettier Cases](gettier-cases.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Suppose the world began five minutes ago, already containing people with apparent memories, half-read books, old buildings, and every present trace of a longer history. Your experience of remembering breakfast would occur now, even though the breakfast itself had never occurred.

Russell uses the possibility to analyze memory: a present memory-like state and the past event it appears to represent are not logically identical. He explicitly does not recommend treating the hypothesis as a serious account of the world. The short timescale makes the distinction vivid; nothing essential depends on exactly five minutes.

## 2. The Central Question

What connects a present state of apparent remembering to an event that really happened?

## 3. Why It Matters

The example separates the contents of current consciousness from their truth conditions. It also distinguishes logical compatibility from explanatory merit. A hypothesis can be constructed to preserve every present trace without thereby offering a good explanation of those traces or a useful program for inquiry.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Analytical reading:** use the case to ask what is present when someone remembers. This is Russell’s stated purpose, rather than an invitation to deny history.

**Skeptical extension:** if present evidence is stipulated to be identical, it cannot deductively exclude the invented past. A stronger account of historical knowledge must therefore explain why deductive exclusion is unnecessary or unavailable.

**Explanatory response:** the ordinary account gives a connected explanation of memories and records through earlier events. The rival is tailored to reproduce the evidence. This is a difference in explanatory support, even if the two stories are logically compatible with a snapshot.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Russell did not argue that the world probably began recently. Nor does the example refute archaeology, history, or memory science. The scenario grants their present evidence and asks a conceptual question about its relationship to the past. Calling a hypothesis unfalsifiable is not by itself a positive reason to accept it.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does knowing a historical fact require proving that every compatible rival is impossible?
2. What distinguishes a memory from a vivid but false apparent memory?
3. Can two hypotheses fit the same evidence while differing in explanatory value?
4. Would moving the creation time from five minutes to five years change the argument or only its emotional impact?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

A world created yesterday, or a person supplied with fabricated autobiographical memories, changes the scale of the same challenge. Person-specific memory alterations also raise identity and responsibility questions, but those are additional issues, not consequences Russell established in the memory passage.
