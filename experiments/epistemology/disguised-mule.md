# The Cleverly Disguised Mule

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Fred Dretske  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Dretske, “Epistemic Operators” (Journal of Philosophy, 1970)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** closure, skepticism, knowledge  
**Related experiments:** [Fake-Barn Country](fake-barn.md); [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md); [Gettier Cases](gettier-cases.md)

## 1. The Scenario

You are at the zoo, standing before a pen labelled “zebras.” The animals look just like zebras, and you say you know that they are zebras. Now consider an implication of that claim: if they are zebras, they are not mules cleverly painted to look like zebras.

Do you know that these animals are not cleverly disguised mules? You have not examined them closely or tested their paint, and your visual evidence is the same whether they are zebras or well-disguised mules.

## 2. The Central Question

If you know something, and you know that it implies something else, must you also know the implied claim?

## 3. Why It Matters

The principle of closure under known entailment says yes. It is intuitive and it underlies much everyday reasoning. But it combines with skeptical hypotheses to yield skepticism: if you must know that you are not a disguised mule, or a brain in a vat, in order to know ordinary things, and you cannot know that, then you do not know ordinary things.

Dretske’s example uses a mundane case to make that structure vivid and to motivate rejecting closure.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Deniers of closure** (Dretske, Nozick) say you can know it is a zebra without knowing it is not a disguised mule. **Contextualists** say the standards for “know” shift: in ordinary talk you know, but raising the possibility of disguise makes the standards stricter. **Moorean replies** say you know the animals are not mules because you know they are zebras. **Skeptics** accept that you do not know either.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The case is not a claim that zoos paint mules, nor that you should doubt your senses. It is a way to isolate a logical principle. Nor does rejecting closure mean rejecting all inference: the dispute is over a specific principle of knowledge.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Do you know the animals are not disguised mules? What would it take?
2. If you deny closure, how do you explain the oddness of saying “I know it’s a zebra but I don’t know it’s not a mule”?
3. Does the answer change if the zoo has a policy against deception?
4. How are mules and brains in vats alike, and how do they differ?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Replacing the mule with a brain in a vat produces a familiar skeptical argument. Dretske’s original paper discusses other operators, such as “reason to believe,” where closure may fail differently.
