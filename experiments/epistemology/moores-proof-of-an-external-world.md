# Moore’s “Here Is One Hand”

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** G. E. Moore  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Moore, “Proof of an External World” (Proceedings of the British Academy, 1939)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** common sense, skepticism, certainty  
**Related experiments:** [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md); [Descartes’s Evil Demon](evil-demon.md); [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Before an audience, Moore raises one hand and says, “Here is one hand.” He raises the other and says, “And here is another.” From these two statements, he deduces that there are at least two external objects, and therefore that external things exist.

The argument is valid: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be. Moore insists that he knows the premises to be true.

## 2. The Central Question

Can a simple, obvious claim such as “here is a hand” be more certain than the philosophical premises used to doubt it?

## 3. Why It Matters

The proof turns the usual structure of skeptical argument upside down. Skeptics start from premises about dreaming or deception and conclude that we do not know ordinary things. Moore argues that we are more certain of ordinary things than of the skeptic’s premises, so if there is a conflict, the premises should go.

The case also provokes debate over what a proof is for. Does a proof need to persuade someone who doubts its premises, or is it enough that it is valid and its premises are known?

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Moorean defenders** say the argument shows that skeptical philosophical premises are less plausible than common-sense knowledge. **Critics** say it begs the question against the skeptic, because the premises already presuppose an external world. **Epistemic-transmission views** argue that a proof can fail to transmit warrant when the premise’s justification depends on the conclusion. **Contextualists** distinguish ordinary standards from philosophical ones.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Moore is not claiming the skeptic can be refuted by showing a hand, and the argument is not a joke. He argues that the reasoning is formally valid and that the premises are known. The question is whether that is enough for a satisfying answer to skepticism.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Do you know you have hands? How confident are you compared with any philosophical premise?
2. What would a skeptic reply, and why might Moore’s answer fail to satisfy?
3. Is a valid argument with known premises always a good proof?
4. Could the dream argument undermine Moore’s premise?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Moore’s “Defence of Common Sense” lists many things he claims to know with certainty. Neo-Moorean views in contemporary epistemology revise the project using ideas about closure and contextual standards.
