# Moore’s “Here Is One Hand”

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** G. E. Moore  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Moore, “Proof of an External World,” *Proceedings of the British Academy* 25 (1939), 273–300
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** common sense, skepticism, certainty  
**Related experiments:** [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md); [Descartes’s Evil Demon](evil-demon.md); [The Dream Argument](dream-argument.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In his 1939 lecture, Moore raises his hands, gestures to one and says, “Here is one hand,” then gestures to the other and adds, “and here is another.” He takes these demonstrative claims—the presence of a hand in each indicated place—as premises for the conclusion that two human hands exist at that moment. Because he counts hands as external objects, their existence establishes that external objects exist.

Moore calls this a rigorous proof. He says a proof must have a premise different from its conclusion, a premise the speaker knows, and a conclusion that follows from the premise. He argues that his demonstration satisfies all three: the specific, gesturally indicated claims say more than the conclusion that two hands exist; he says he knows those claims; and if two hands are there, it follows that two hands exist. Whether the premises meet Moore’s own standard does not by itself settle whether a skeptic should accept them.

## 2. The Central Question

Does a valid argument from premises Moore says he knows answer someone who doubts that we know anything about the external world?

## 3. Why It Matters

The hand proof is often presented as a direct response to skepticism. Keep two claims distinct: Moore argues that external objects exist, and he says he knows the hand-premises; his demonstration does not, by itself, prove the separate claim that nobody is deceived or dreaming, or that a skeptic must accept that he knows the premises. In the lecture’s closing discussion, Moore acknowledges that he has not proved the hand-premises and says he cannot provide a proof that he is not dreaming. In a later reply, he likewise distinguishes denying that material things exist from denying that anyone knows material things exist.

This helps explain why the proof can seem compelling as a deduction yet fail to persuade a skeptical audience. The dispute is partly about what a proof must establish: a valid inference from premises its author claims to know, or premises and reasoning that also answer the skeptic’s doubt.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**A proof of existence, not a proof of knowledge:** Thomas Baldwin’s Stanford Encyclopedia account reads Moore’s stated aim as proving that external objects exist, not proving that he knows they exist or refuting the claim that no one knows material things exist. Baldwin also argues that the hand demonstration does not settle the further metaphysical question of whether such objects are wholly independent of experience or thought.

**A different reading of Moore’s stated aim:** Charles Raff argues that Moore’s lecture aimed to counterexample a presupposition in Kant’s problem of the external world, rather than to solve that problem or refute skepticism in the usual sense. This is a scholarly interpretation, not a settled consensus; Raff explicitly challenges the standard reading.

**The skeptical challenge and later debates:** A familiar objection is that Moore’s premise begs the question against a skeptic who doubts perception or the existence of hands. In contemporary epistemology, philosophers debate whether this is a failure to transmit justification or instead a failure to settle the skeptic’s doubt. Chris Tucker’s overview describes this as a live disagreement and notes James Pryor’s defense of Moore’s proof against the transmission-failure charge. Epistemic contextualism is a separate family of responses to skeptical arguments: it explains the apparent conflict between ordinary and skeptical knowledge claims by appeal to context-sensitive standards. It is not Moore’s own stated conclusion.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Moore’s proof is not merely a joke or a bare gesture offered as a complete answer to every skeptical challenge. He argues that the inference is rigorous because its premise differs from its conclusion, is known, and entails that conclusion. But he does not establish that his hand-premises are acceptable to a skeptic, and he explicitly says he has not proved them. The proof’s validity, the status of its premises, and its power to answer skepticism are separate questions.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Do you know you have hands? How confident are you compared with any philosophical premise?
2. If you already doubt that you have hands, does Moore’s demonstration give you a reason to change your mind?
3. Is a valid argument with premises its author claims to know always a satisfactory response to a skeptic?
4. What would it take to prove that you are not dreaming—and is that the same task as proving that hands exist?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

In “A Defence of Common Sense” (1925), Moore lists propositions he claims to know with certainty. Contemporary **Neo-Moorean arguments** develop anti-skeptical reasoning that uses ordinary knowledge claims and principles such as epistemic closure; they should not be conflated with the precise proof in the 1939 lecture. **Contextualism** is a distinct approach: it holds that the standards for a true knowledge attribution can vary with context, including when skeptical doubts become prominent.
