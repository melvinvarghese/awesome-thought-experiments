# Goodman’s New Riddle of Induction (Grue)

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Nelson Goodman  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Goodman, Fact, Fiction, and Forecast (1955)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** induction, projectibility, language  
**Related experiments:** [Hempel’s Paradox of the Ravens](ravens-paradox.md); [Bertrand’s Random Chord Paradox](../mathematics/bertrands-paradox.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Every emerald we have examined is green. This seems to support the generalization that all emeralds are green. Now define a new word, “grue.” An object is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue.

Every emerald examined so far is green and was examined before t, so it is also grue. The evidence therefore seems to support both “All emeralds are green” and “All emeralds are grue.” They make different predictions for emeralds examined after t.

## 2. The Central Question

What distinguishes generalizations that our evidence legitimately supports from ones that fit the same evidence but should not be projected?

## 3. Why It Matters

Hume’s old problem asks why past regularities should guide predictions. Goodman’s riddle shows that even if we accept induction, we need to say which regularities to extend. A rule such as “project what has held so far” gives contradictory advice depending on how we describe the data.

It affects machine learning and statistics, where the choice of features or hypothesis class determines which patterns count as natural.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Goodman’s answer** is entrenchment: predicates like green have a record of past projection that grue lacks. **Naturalness views** say that green marks a real similarity in the world while grue is gerrymandered. **Positional objections** say that grue is defined by time, while green is not, though a speaker of a grue-language could reverse the objection. **Bayesian and statistical approaches** address the choice through prior probabilities.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The riddle does not claim that emeralds will change colour, or that science is irrational. It shows that confirmation needs more than logical form. It is also distinct from Hume’s problem: the new riddle assumes induction is acceptable and asks which predicates to use.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is there a non-circular way to say why green is a better predicate than grue?
2. Could a community whose basic colour words were grue and bleen see ours as the odd ones?
3. What does the riddle suggest about choosing variables for a predictive model?
4. How does entrenchment handle genuinely new kinds of evidence?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Goodman also discussed other artificial predicates. Time-indexed versions can be replaced by ones that depend on place, showing that the riddle is not only about time.
