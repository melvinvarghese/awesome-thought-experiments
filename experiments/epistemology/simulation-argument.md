# The Simulation Argument

**Category:** epistemology  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Nick Bostrom  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Bostrom, “Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?” (Philosophical Quarterly, 2003)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** simulation, probability, skepticism  
**Related experiments:** [Brain in a Vat](brain-in-a-vat.md); [Descartes’s Evil Demon](evil-demon.md); [Russell’s Five-Minute World](russells-five-minute-world.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Suppose that technology eventually allows civilizations to run detailed simulations of their ancestors, so detailed that the simulated people have conscious experiences. Suppose also that such simulations could be run in great numbers.

Bostrom argues that, given these assumptions, at least one of three statements must be true: almost all civilizations at our stage go extinct before reaching that technology; almost all civilizations that reach it have no interest in running such simulations; or almost all beings with experiences like ours are simulated.

## 2. The Central Question

Given some assumptions about technology and consciousness, how likely is it that our experiences are those of simulated beings?

## 3. Why It Matters

Unlike Descartes’s demon, the argument is probabilistic and concerns evidence about our future. It makes clear what must be assumed for the skeptical conclusion: that consciousness can arise in an artificial substrate and that simulated minds would greatly outnumber unsimulated ones. If either assumption fails, the argument does not go through.

It also links skeptical themes to forecasting and to the ethics of creating artificial minds.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Acceptance of the trilemma** shifts the debate to which branch is most plausible. **Criticism of the substrate assumption** argues that computation may not suffice for consciousness. **Criticism of the counting argument** says the reasoning about how to assign probability among observers is contested. **Epistemological responses** ask whether being in a simulation would undermine knowledge of the world or just change what the world is. Each depends on assumptions about observers and reference classes.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Bostrom does not claim that we probably are simulated; he claims that one of the three alternatives is true. The argument is also not empirical evidence for simulation, and it does not show that simulated life would be unreal or meaningless. Popular summaries often collapse the trilemma into its third horn.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which of the three alternatives do you find most plausible, and why?
2. Does it matter if you cannot tell whether your world is simulated?
3. What would a simulated being need to have for its experiences to count?
4. How is this different from a brain in a vat?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Versions vary the reference class used for probability, or ask about simulations of partial worlds. Each choice affects the numbers, so it should be stated explicitly.
