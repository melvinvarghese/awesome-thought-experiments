# Repugnant Conclusion

**Category:** ethics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Derek Parfit, Henry Sidgwick (precursor)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Reasons and Persons (1984); earlier aggregation concerns predate Parfit  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** population ethics, aggregation, quality of life  
**Related experiments:** nonidentity-problem, utility-monster  
**Kind:** argument  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

Compare a population in which everyone lives a very good life with a much larger population in which every life is only barely worth living. If value is total welfare and each additional life adds a small positive amount, a sufficiently enormous population of the latter kind has a greater sum. The disturbing conclusion is that this larger population can be ranked better, other relevant things being equal.

## 2. The Central Question

Can enough minimally good lives make a world better than one containing fewer excellent lives?

## 3. Why It Matters

The issue concerns how ethical evaluation works when both the number and identity of people change. Unlike ordinary distribution problems, there need not be a fixed set of individuals whose welfare can simply be compared before and after a choice.

As an illustration, one hundred people each at welfare level one hundred yield a total of ten thousand. Twenty thousand people each at level one yield twenty thousand. A total-based ranking favors the second population even though each life is much less good. These invented units clarify a conditional implication rather than measuring real lives. The philosophical disagreement remains even when the arithmetic is correct: should quality, distribution, rights, or the distinction between creating benefits and benefiting existing people alter the comparison? State which element of the ranking you reject.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Total-welfare approaches accept the mathematical implication or question the reliability of intuitions about enormous populations. Average-welfare approaches resist it but face other objections, including how adding happy people can lower an average. Critical-level and pluralist approaches seek alternatives, with further tradeoffs rather than an uncontested solution.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Barely worth living means positive overall welfare, not a life that is worse than nonexistence. The argument does not assume that present population growth actually improves welfare. Parfit made the problem prominent; he was not the first thinker to notice tensions between quantity and quality.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What exactly makes a life barely worth living?
2. Can you reject the conclusion while accepting that adding a happy life never worsens a world?
3. Should population rankings alone determine what anyone is obligated to do?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The mere-addition argument reaches related conclusions through a sequence of comparisons that can each seem plausible. Distinguish that argumentative route from the direct arithmetic implication of a total-welfare formula.
