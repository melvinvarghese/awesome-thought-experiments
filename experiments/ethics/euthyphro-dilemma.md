# The Euthyphro Dilemma

**Category:** ethics  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Plato (Socrates in the dialogue Euthyphro)  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Plato, Euthyphro (fourth century BCE)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** divine command, moral objectivity, grounds of obligation  
**Related experiments:** [The Ring of Gyges](../society-and-justice/ring-of-gyges.md); [The Veil of Ignorance and Original Position](../society-and-justice/veil-of-ignorance.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In Plato’s dialogue, Socrates meets Euthyphro, who is confident he knows what piety is. Socrates asks a question that has since been generalized: is what is pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because the gods love it?

Replace “pious” with “morally good” and “the gods” with “God,” and the question becomes: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

## 2. The Central Question

Does a moral standard depend on the will of a divine being, or does it hold independently of any will, including God’s?

## 3. Why It Matters

If the good is good only because it is commanded, then it seems that anything, however cruel, would be good had it been commanded, and divine goodness risks becoming empty. If God commands it because it is good, then goodness has a source independent of God, and morality no longer depends on divine will for its content.

The dilemma matters beyond theology. It asks whether any moral standard can be both authoritative and non-arbitrary, a question that arises for social conventions, laws and natural-law theories alike.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Divine command theorists** accept the first horn but argue that God’s nature is essentially good, so commands are not arbitrary. **Natural-law and rationalist views** accept the second horn: reasons for action are available to human reason and God’s commands track them. **Modified approaches** locate goodness in God’s nature, so the choice is a false dichotomy. **Secular critics** use the argument to show that moral authority does not require a divine source. These replies differ in whether they accept the dilemma’s assumptions.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The dilemma is not a proof that God does not exist, nor does it show that religious ethics is irrational. It identifies a structural problem, and the major traditions have long-developed answers. In Plato’s dialogue, the topic is piety and the gods of Athenian religion, not a single monotheistic deity; the later formulation is a use of Plato’s question.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which horn do you think is more costly, and what exactly makes it costly?
2. Can you state the same dilemma for a human lawgiver or a society’s customs?
3. If God’s nature is good, is that a standard distinct from God’s will?
4. Does your answer change what a person could learn about morality from religion?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The same structure applies to other sources of authority: Is something right because a majority approves, or does the majority approve because it is right? These analogues share the logic but differ in the plausibility of each horn.
