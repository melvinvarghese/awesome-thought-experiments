# Trolley Problem

**Category:** ethics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Philippa Foot, Judith Jarvis Thomson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Foot, The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect (1967); Thomson’s later developments  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** killing, intention, consequences  
**Related experiments:** transplant, violinist  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

A runaway trolley is heading toward five people who cannot escape. You can divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one person. For this simplified version, assume the numbers and outcomes are certain and there is no way to stop the trolley or warn anyone. The puzzle becomes sharper when compared with pushing a person from a footbridge to stop the trolley: five are still saved, but the person’s body is now part of the rescue mechanism.

## 2. The Central Question

When, if ever, may saving several lives justify foreseeably killing one?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case tests the relationship between consequences, rights, intentions, and using someone as a means. A decision alone is not yet a theory: the task is to identify a principle that explains why similar numerical outcomes can receive different judgments.

To test a proposed rule, write it without mentioning trolleys, then apply it to both the switch and footbridge versions. If the rule changes, identify the new morally relevant feature. A rule such as “save the larger number” is clear but may generate uncomfortable implications elsewhere. A rule such as “never cause harm” must explain why diverting the trolley counts differently from declining to divert it. This exercise reveals whether your judgment rests on a stable principle or on a feature of the story whose relevance you have not yet defended.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Consequentialist approaches emphasize the better overall outcome, although their treatment of rules and uncertainty varies. Rights-based and double-effect approaches ask whether the death is intended as a means or merely foreseen. Thomson’s comparisons challenge overly simple versions of either distinction.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Foot’s original driver case and later bystander-switch versions are not historically identical. The footbridge case is a significant variation, not a second independent discovery. A classroom answer does not establish how anyone would behave in an emergency.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does intention change the victim’s complaint, or only the agent’s character?
2. Would your principle allow killing an unwilling organ donor?
3. Which certainty assumption matters most when applying the case outside the classroom?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Thomson’s footbridge and loop-track variants test whether the victim is used as a means. Changing probabilities, consent, or special responsibilities creates further problems, but those changes must be stated rather than silently imported.
