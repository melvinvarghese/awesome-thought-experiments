# Jim and the Captain (the Integrity Objection)

**Category:** ethics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Bernard Williams, J. J. C. Smart  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Smart and Williams, Utilitarianism: For and Against (1973), Williams’s part  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** integrity, consequentialism, responsibility  
**Related experiments:** [Trolley Problem](trolley.md); [Transplant Problem](transplant.md); [Utility Monster](utility-monster.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Jim, a traveller, arrives in a small town where an army captain is about to execute twenty captives chosen at random as a warning. As a visitor, Jim is offered a favour: if he personally shoots one of the captives, the captain will release the other nineteen. If Jim refuses, all twenty will be killed. Assume that Jim cannot stop the executions by force, that the captain will keep his word, and that no one else can intervene.

A simple consequentialist reckoning says Jim should shoot: one death is better than twenty. Williams argues that this answer is too quick.

## 2. The Central Question

Do a person’s deepest commitments and sense of integrity count for something in deciding what to do, or must they be weighed like any other preference in an impartial calculation?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case targets a particular feature of act utilitarianism: it treats my action as one cause among others, so what I do is just a way of bringing about the best total outcome, whoever performs it. Williams objects that this fails to respect the fact that actions are done by persons who have projects and commitments that make them who they are. He also suggests that the demand to maximize can alienate people from their own moral lives.

The case is useful because it separates two questions: what outcome is best, and what an agent may be required to do in order to bring it about.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Standard consequentialists** reply that, in Jim’s situation, shooting is right, and that a feeling of reluctance is an understandable but not decisive reaction. **Sophisticated consequentialists** argue that a good moral character, including firm commitments, has good effects overall. **Williams’s own position** is not that Jim must refuse; he doubts that the utilitarian can make sense of the thought that this is an agonizing choice for the person who must make it. **Deontological and agent-relative views** allow constraints or prerogatives that limit what may be demanded of an agent.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument is not that integrity always overrides saving lives, and Williams does not claim that Jim is certainly wrong to shoot. The point is about what kind of reasoning the theory permits Jim to treat as relevant. The case also should not be read as an empirical claim about how any real person would behave.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. If you think Jim should shoot, does your reasoning leave room for his sense of what he can live with?
2. Would your view change if Jim were required to shoot ten captives to save a hundred?
3. Is there a morally relevant difference between Jim killing one and the captain killing nineteen?
4. Can a theory both give weight to integrity and avoid letting people refuse any difficult duty by appeal to it?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Williams pairs Jim with a second case about a chemist with qualms about work on weapons. Altering who the captives are, how certain the offer is, or whether Jim has promised something changes the structure of the case, so those changes need to be stated.
