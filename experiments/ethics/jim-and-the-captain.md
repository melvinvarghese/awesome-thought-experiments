# Jim and the Captain (the Integrity Objection)

**Category:** ethics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Bernard Williams (author of “A Critique of Utilitarianism”); J. J. C. Smart (co-author of the volume)\
**Original publication or approximate period:** Smart and Williams, *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), Williams’s part\
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** integrity, consequentialism, responsibility  
**Related experiments:** [Trolley Problem](trolley.md); [Transplant Problem](transplant.md); [Utility Monster](utility-monster.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Jim, a traveller, arrives in a small town where an army captain is about to execute twenty captives chosen at random as a warning. The captain offers a choice: if Jim personally shoots one captive, the other nineteen will be released; if Jim refuses, all twenty will be killed. Williams says that Jim’s own attempt to overpower the captain and soldiers would fail. For this retelling, assume the captain honors the offer and no third party can intervene; the latter is an added constraint, not stated explicitly by Williams.

A direct utilitarian reckoning says Jim should shoot under those conditions: one death is better than twenty. Williams argues that this answer is too quick.

## 2. The Central Question

Do a person’s deepest commitments and sense of integrity count for something in deciding what to do, or must they be weighed like any other preference in an impartial calculation?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case targets a feature of act utilitarianism: it can treat an agent’s action as one causal route among others toward the best overall outcome. Williams objects that this can fail to respect the fact that actions are done by people whose projects and commitments help make them who they are. He also argues that the demand to maximize can alienate people from their own moral lives.

The case separates two questions: what outcome is best, and what an agent may be required to do in order to bring it about.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Williams says utilitarianism recommends shooting under the case’s stated conditions, but he does not present Jim’s choice as a simple example in which the theory gives the wrong answer. Interpretations of the integrity objection differ. Some direct act-utilitarian approaches focus on the outcome calculation; indirect or agent-relative consequentialist theories may respond differently, and other theories add constraints or prerogatives. These are examples of possible approaches, not an exhaustive taxonomy or a settled consensus.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument is not that integrity always overrides saving lives, and Williams does not claim that Jim is certainly wrong to shoot. The point concerns what kind of reasoning the theory permits Jim to treat as relevant. The case is not an empirical claim about how any real person would behave.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. If you think Jim should shoot, does your reasoning leave room for his sense of what he can live with?
2. Would your view change if Jim were required to shoot ten captives to save a hundred?
3. Is there a morally relevant difference between Jim killing one and the captain killing nineteen?
4. Can a theory both give weight to integrity and avoid letting people refuse any difficult duty by appeal to it?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Williams pairs Jim with a second case about a chemist who has qualms about work on weapons. Altering who the captives are, how certain the offer is, or whether Jim has promised something changes the structure of the case; these are teaching prompts, not descriptions of the original text.
