# The Terror Bomber and the Tactical Bomber

## 1. The Scenario

In Alison McIntyre’s example, the terror bomber aims to kill civilians to weaken enemy resolve, while the tactical bomber aims at a military target and foresees civilian deaths. For comparison, this article stipulates that both plans would certainly cause the same number of civilian deaths. This is a philosophical hypothetical, not a report about an actual air campaign.

## 2. The Central Question

Does it matter morally whether harm is intended as a means or merely foreseen as a side effect when both plans are stipulated to cause the same number of civilian deaths?

## 3. Why It Matters

McIntyre describes the contrast as matching a widely affirmed judgment in the stipulated case. That reports a judgment discussed in the entry; it does not establish universal agreement or by itself settle whether an attack is permissible. The doctrine of double effect is one proposed account, and its formulations include conditions beyond the intention/side-effect distinction.

The example also raises questions about wartime civilian harm. The cited SEP entry discusses humanitarian-law precautions and medical ethics, but it does not establish a general legal test for every conflict or policing situation.

## 4. Major Interpretations

McIntyre discusses several distinct disagreements. Some critics question whether intention has independent moral significance; others dispute how the doctrine applies to particular cases or whether it explains the judgments it is meant to capture. One account, associated with Quinn, distinguishes direct from indirect agency. The entry also discusses views on which intentions can matter to deliberation or moral assessment even when their effect on permissibility is contested. These are selected positions, not a settled or exhaustive taxonomy.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Foreseeing harm does not by itself make an act permissible. McIntyre’s discussion treats proportionality as insufficient on its own and also emphasizes minimizing harm and considering feasible precautions. The example is a philosophical analysis, not a legal verdict that a particular bombing is lawful or justified.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Where exactly does the moral difference lie: in the act, the victim’s complaint, or the pilot’s character?
2. If the pilot could hide the true aim, would the act change?
3. Is it enough that harm is a side effect if it is certain and enormous?
4. How would you test whether your reaction depends on intention or on some other feature of the story?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Discussions of the trolley problem use the doctrine to compare redirecting a threat with using a person as a means. Varying the number of civilians or the likelihood of success changes the hypothetical; these are teaching prompts, not claims about the cited bomber example.
