# The Terror Bomber and the Tactical Bomber

**Category:** ethics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** No single originator is named in the sources consulted  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Developed in twentieth-century discussion of the doctrine of double effect, which has medieval roots  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** intention, double effect, war  
**Related experiments:** [Trolley Problem](trolley.md); [Transplant Problem](transplant.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Two pilots serve in a war that one may assume is just. The tactical bomber attacks a munitions factory, knowing that civilians living nearby will be killed as a side effect. The terror bomber attacks civilian neighbourhoods on purpose in order to break the enemy’s morale and end the war sooner.

Suppose, for the sake of the case, that the same number of civilians would die in each plan and that both are equally likely to shorten the war.

## 2. The Central Question

Does it matter morally whether harm is intended as a means or merely foreseen as a side effect, when the outcomes are otherwise the same?

## 3. Why It Matters

The doctrine of double effect says, roughly, that it can be permissible to bring about a serious harm as a foreseen side effect that would be impermissible to bring about as a means. The two bombers are used to show that this distinction seems to match a widely shared judgment about war: attacking non-combatants as a tool is treated as far worse than causing collateral damage.

The case matters for law and ethics in conflict, medicine and policing, where it is often said that an agent’s aim, not just the result, changes the nature of an act.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Defenders of double effect** say that intending harm as a means treats the victim as an instrument, while a side effect does not; additional conditions such as proportionality are required in both cases. **Critics** argue that, with outcomes fixed, the distinction does no independent moral work or that it is hard to apply because descriptions of intention can be manipulated. **Hybrid views** hold that intention affects the quality of the agent’s will without changing what is permissible, or the reverse.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The pair does not show that all collateral damage is acceptable: the tactical bombing must still be proportionate and necessary. It also does not show that intention is the only factor. Another common error is to treat the doctrine as a decision procedure rather than as one proposed explanation of why some cases feel different.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Where exactly does the moral difference lie: in the act, the victim’s complaint, or the pilot’s character?
2. If the pilot could hide the true aim, would the act change?
3. Is it enough that harm is a side effect if it is certain and enormous?
4. How would you test whether your reaction depends on intention or on some other feature of the story?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Discussions of the trolley problem use the doctrine to compare redirecting a threat with using a person as a means. Variations that vary the numbers of civilians or the likelihood of success change the proportionality question, not only the intention question.
