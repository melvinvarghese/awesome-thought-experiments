# Experience Machine

**Category:** ethics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Robert Nozick  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974); revised presentation in The Examined Life (1989)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** well-being, authenticity, pleasure  
**Related experiments:** marys-room, violinist  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

A machine can supply experiences as convincing and satisfying as the best events of an ordinary life. You could seem to create important art, sustain friendships, and accomplish difficult projects. While connected, you would not remember choosing a simulation. Put aside malfunction and unwanted suffering. Compare a life with these experiences to an otherwise less pleasurable life containing actual achievements and relationships.

## 2. The Central Question

Is the quality of experience all that makes a life good for its subject?

## 3. Why It Matters

The machine isolates the difference between seeming to do something and actually doing it. It challenges experiential accounts of welfare, while also asking how much confidence to place in intuitive choices involving unfamiliar technology.

Try making two comparisons. First, hold the amount of pleasure fixed and vary whether achievements are real. Second, hold achievements fixed and vary pleasure. If both changes matter, your account of welfare contains more than a single experiential dimension. Then ask whether your reluctance to enter concerns your own welfare or a duty to existing friends. The machine is meant to illuminate the former question. A duty to someone outside the machine may justify refusing it without establishing that the simulated life would be worse for its occupant.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Nozick takes reluctance to connect as evidence that doing, being a certain person, and contact with reality matter independently of experience. Hedonists may question the inference: reluctance could reflect fear, status quo bias, duties to others, or uncertainty rather than the intrinsic value of reality. Reversed scenarios investigate these possibilities.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument is not a demonstration that all virtual experiences lack value. A simulation may contain real social relationships, unlike a solitary machine supplying appearances. Nozick’s 1974 and 1989 presentations differ; a lifelong version should not be presented as the only original wording.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Would you leave if you discovered you were already connected?
2. Can an achievement be good for you if you never feel pleased by it?
3. Which obligations would need to be removed to isolate your own welfare?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Reversed experience-machine cases begin with the reader already connected. Experience-pill versions and cases with real interaction help separate pleasure, authenticity, and attachment to familiar circumstances.
