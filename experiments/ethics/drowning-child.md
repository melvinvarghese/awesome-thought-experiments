# Drowning Child

**Category:** ethics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Peter Singer  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Famine, Affluence, and Morality (1972)  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** beneficence, distance, demandingness  
**Related experiments:** violinist, utility-monster  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

You pass a shallow pond and see a child drowning. You can rescue the child safely, but your clothes will be spoiled. The inconvenience is trivial compared with the child’s death. Singer uses this case to support a principle requiring prevention of serious harm when doing so does not require a sacrifice of comparable moral importance. He then asks why geographical distance should change our obligations to people facing severe deprivation.

## 2. The Central Question

How far does an easy duty of rescue extend beyond people immediately in front of us?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case connects a familiar rescue judgment to much more demanding obligations. It forces readers to distinguish practical differences in information and effectiveness from the bare fact that another person lives far away.

Separate the argument into a rescue judgment, a general principle explaining it, and a proposed application to distant assistance. You can accept the first while disputing either of the next steps, but then you owe an account of the difference. For example, uncertainty about whether help reaches someone is relevant in a way that distance alone may not be. This structure also makes criticism more precise: showing that one organization is ineffective does not show that no obligation to assist exists, just as accepting rescue duties does not identify the best institution.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Singer argues that distance and the presence of other potential helpers do not by themselves erase an obligation. Critics may accept local rescue duties while defending agent-centered permissions, special obligations, limits on demandingness, or institutional approaches to injustice. Disagreement can concern the proposed principle or its application.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The original simple example involves muddy clothes; elaborate shoes and price tags are later presentations. The analogy does not establish that every donation succeeds. Actual aid decisions require evidence about effectiveness, whereas the rescue premise makes success straightforward.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does seeing someone’s face change your duty or merely your motivation?
2. When other helpers refuse to act, does your fair share set a moral ceiling?
3. Which differences between rescue and distant assistance are morally relevant?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Cases with several bystanders test whether responsibility is diluted. Iterated rescues test demandingness: many individually small sacrifices can eventually transform a life. These extensions must be argued through rather than assumed to follow without cost.
