# The Violinist

**Category:** ethics  
**Associated thinker(s):** Judith Jarvis Thomson  
**Original publication or approximate period:** A Defense of Abortion (1971)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** bodily autonomy, rights, obligation  
**Related experiments:** drowning-child, transplant  
**Kind:** thought experiment  
**Research checked:** 2026-10-30

## 1. The Scenario

You wake up connected to a celebrated unconscious violinist. A group has kidnapped you because only your kidneys can support him. Disconnecting now would end his life; after nine months he will recover and no longer need you. You did not agree to the connection. Thomson asks us to grant, for argument’s sake, that the dependent individual has a right to life, then examine what follows about his entitlement to use another person’s body.

## 2. The Central Question

Does a right to life automatically include a right to another person’s bodily support?

## 3. Why It Matters

The scenario separates moral status from the content and limits of rights. It also separates actions that are generous from actions that others may demand. Its role in an abortion argument depends on an analogy, so identifying morally relevant similarities and differences is part of the reasoning.

A useful analysis distinguishes three propositions: the violinist’s life matters; continuing support would benefit him; he is entitled to require it. The first two do not logically entail the third without an additional principle about obligations. Conversely, rejecting an entitlement does not mean that withdrawing support is admirable in every circumstance. Specify both what the dependent person can demand and what the supporting person has reason to offer voluntarily. This keeps the argument from sliding between the language of rights, duties, kindness, and overall moral assessment as if they meant the same thing.

## 4. Major Interpretations

Thomson argues that a person may lack an enforceable right to bodily support even when support is necessary to remain alive. Critics question whether parental responsibilities, voluntary risk-taking, or the difference between killing and withdrawing aid make pregnancy importantly different. Those objections address distinct versions of the analogy.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Thomson does not conclude that every abortion is permissible or that dependents have no moral claims. The kidnapping premise is especially relevant to nonconsensual dependence. This philosophical case is not clinical or legal advice about any actual pregnancy.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What can another person demand from your body rather than merely request?
2. Would one hour of support differ from nine months, and why?
3. What actions, if any, generate a special duty to continue support?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Thomson also discusses people-seeds, the expanding child, and very short periods of assistance. These variations examine responsibility, self-defense, and minimal decency; they should not be collapsed into the kidnapping case.
