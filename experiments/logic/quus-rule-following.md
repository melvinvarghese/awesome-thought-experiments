# Kripke’s Quus

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Saul Kripke, Ludwig Wittgenstein  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Kripke, Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language (1982), reading Wittgenstein’s Philosophical Investigations  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** rule-following, meaning, skepticism  
**Related experiments:** [Goodman’s New Riddle of Induction (Grue)](../epistemology/grue.md); [Beetle in a Box](../consciousness/beetle-in-box.md)

## 1. The Scenario

You have added many numbers in your life, but never any two numbers as large as 57. Now you are asked, “What is 68 plus 57?” You answer 125 and are confident this is correct.

A skeptic now suggests that by “plus” you always meant a different function, “quus.” For numbers smaller than 57, quus gives the same answer as plus. For larger numbers, quus gives 5. According to the skeptic, your answer of 125 is a mistake: your past use is equally compatible with your having meant quus, so the right answer is 5.

## 2. The Central Question

What fact about you, past or present, makes it the case that you meant plus and not quus?

## 3. Why It Matters

The case appears absurd, but the challenge is serious. Every candidate fact seems to fail: your past uses are finite and compatible with both; your mental images do not determine a function; dispositions are finite and can err. If no fact fixes meaning, then there is no such thing as meaning something by a word, and the very idea of following a rule becomes puzzling.

Kripke presents this as Wittgenstein’s skeptical paradox about rules and proposes a “skeptical solution” based on communal practices.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Kripke’s skeptical solution** says that attributions of meaning are justified by community agreement, not by facts about individuals. **Dispositionalists** say that meaning is fixed by what you are disposed to answer, if idealized. **Primitivists** say meaning facts are basic. **Rejecting the interpretation** says Kripke misreads Wittgenstein. These disputes are central to the philosophy of language and mind.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument is not about arithmetic, and no one thinks that 68 plus 57 is 5. It is about whether any fact makes our meaning determinate. It is also not the same as the grue puzzle, though both involve a deviant extension of a rule that fits past data. Attribution of the argument to Wittgenstein himself is disputed.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What fact about your past makes it the case that you meant plus?
2. Why can’t you just say you meant the addition function?
3. Does the community view solve the problem or relocate it?
4. Does the argument apply to your own present thoughts?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The skeptic’s move can be generalized to any word, which is why the argument is so general. Comparing it with Goodman’s grue and Quine’s indeterminacy shows how three different problems share a pattern.
