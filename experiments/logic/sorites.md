# Sorites: When Does a Heap Begin?

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Eubulides of Miletus  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Greek tradition, fourth century BCE; attribution traditional  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** vagueness, logic, classification  
**Related experiments:** [The Liar Paradox](liar.md); [The Unexpected Hanging / Surprise Test](unexpected-hanging.md)

## 1. The Scenario

One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding one grain seems too small a change to turn a non-heap into a heap. Repeating this reasoning, two grains are not a heap, nor three, nor four. Continue far enough and the argument says that even a very large pile is not a heap.

The reverse argument begins with an obvious heap and removes grains. Both versions rely on a tolerance assumption: a single small change never makes the decisive difference. The puzzle is a paradox about vague predicates, rather than a scientific hypothesis about the behavior of sand.

## 2. The Central Question

Which commitment should be revised when small-step reasoning conflicts with clear endpoint judgments?

## 3. Why It Matters

Vague terms remain useful even without an agreed sharp boundary. The paradox makes that usefulness intellectually puzzling. It also encourages care when institutions replace ordinary categories with numerical thresholds: defining a legal cutoff may solve an administrative problem without revealing a boundary already present in ordinary language.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Epistemicism:** there is a precise boundary, but we cannot know exactly where it lies. The tolerance assumption is false even though it feels compelling.

**Supervaluationism:** admissible ways of making the word precise disagree on borderline cases. Some statements can remain true under every acceptable sharpening without a uniquely privileged cutoff.

**Degree-based approaches:** borderline applications may have intermediate truth values, with corresponding changes to how the argument is evaluated. These approaches disagree in their logical details; calling all of them “fuzzy” hides significant differences.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

No particular number of grains solves every ordinary use of “heap.” But the absence of a known cutoff does not automatically prove that there cannot be one. Nor is every gradual physical process a sorites paradox: the argument needs a predicate, contrasting endpoints, and a small-change premise that produces the trouble.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is “one grain makes no difference” a claim about perception, meaning, or truth?
2. Would a legislated cutoff explain what “heap” already meant?
3. Can a sentence be unsettled even though a disjunction containing it is definitely true?
4. Which cost seems smaller: an unknowable boundary or revising familiar logic?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Baldness, color boundaries, and becoming old supply familiar variants. Moral and identity cases also use gradual changes, but their controversial premises must be examined separately. A slow change does not guarantee that the same semantic diagnosis applies.
