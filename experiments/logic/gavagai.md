# Quine’s Gavagai

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** W. V. O. Quine  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Quine, Word and Object (1960)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** radical translation, indeterminacy, reference  
**Related experiments:** [Beetle in a Box](../consciousness/beetle-in-box.md); [Hesperus and Phosphorus](hesperus-and-phosphorus.md); [Kripke’s Quus](quus-rule-following.md)

## 1. The Scenario

A linguist arrives among speakers of an unknown language with no interpreter and no dictionary. A rabbit runs by and a native speaker says “Gavagai.” The linguist guesses that it means “Rabbit.” This is the best hypothesis given the evidence.

Quine points out that the evidence also fits other translations: “undetached rabbit part,” “rabbit stage,” or “a stretch of rabbit-ness.” All of these would be triggered by the same stimulations, and no amount of pointing or further observation seems to rule them out without already assuming a translation of other words.

## 2. The Central Question

Is there a fact of the matter about what a word refers to, beyond what is settled by how speakers behave?

## 3. Why It Matters

Quine argues that translation is indeterminate: there can be two translation manuals, each consistent with all the behavioural evidence, that are incompatible with each other. If meaning is determined by use and behaviour, then there may be no fact that decides between them. This challenges the idea that each word has a determinate meaning waiting to be discovered.

The thesis bears on translation, interpretation of people and animals, and theories of mental content.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Quine’s own view** is that reference is inscrutable and meaning is indeterminate. **Critics** reply that additional constraints, such as simplicity and charity, or facts about psychology, narrow the options. **Externalist and causal views** argue that reference is fixed by causal connections, not behaviour alone. **Pragmatic readings** treat the thesis as a limitation on what linguists can learn, not a metaphysical claim.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

Quine is not saying that translation is impossible, or that people never understand each other. The claim is about the underdetermination of translation by evidence. The “undetached rabbit part” hypothesis is not meant to be plausible but to show that the evidence leaves room for it.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What further evidence could distinguish “rabbit” from “undetached rabbit part”?
2. Do you think a child learning English faces the same problem?
3. Is indeterminacy of translation different from ordinary uncertainty?
4. Does your answer depend on whether meaning is in the head?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Kripke’s Wittgenstein poses a related challenge about rule-following, and Davidson’s radical interpretation proposes methods for narrowing the possibilities.
