# Zeno’s Arrow

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Zeno of Elea  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Greek, fifth century BCE; known through later reports  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** motion, instants, continuity  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Dichotomy](zenos-dichotomy.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Aristotle reports Zeno as arguing that, if whatever occupies a space equal to itself is at rest and a moving thing is always in a “now,” the flying arrow is motionless (*Physics* VI.9, 239b5–9; repeated at 239b30–33). A familiar reconstruction fills in the reasoning: at each instant the arrow occupies one position and travels no distance during that durationless instant; if its flight consists only of such instants, it seems to be at rest at every one and never to move. The surviving account is Aristotle’s report, not Zeno’s own wording. Aristotle says the argument depends on treating time as composed of indivisible moments.

## 2. The Central Question

If a moving object is at rest at each instant, what makes it a moving object?

## 3. Why It Matters

The paradox focuses on what motion consists in. In calculus, instantaneous velocity is defined as the limit of average velocity over shorter and shorter time intervals. An “at-at” account describes motion through an object’s occupying different positions at different times, rather than through a further change occurring within a durationless instant. Whether this captures everything involved in motion remains a philosophical question; this is a question for discussion, not a claim that the ancient argument settles it.

It is also a test case for views about time: whether time is made of instants, and whether the present has any duration.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Aristotle’s response** rejects the premise that time is composed of indivisible moments (*Physics* VI.9, 239b8–9). **A modern response** allows an account in terms of instants but rejects the inference that occupying one position at an instant shows that the arrow is at rest. The at-at account describes motion as being at different places at different times. These are responses to the paradox, not claims that Zeno’s lost text states them or that one account is the only possible theory of motion.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox is not a direct observation that arrows never move; it presses a question about how motion is possible. The modern calculus response uses a limit of changes across intervals and does not require an object to travel a distance during a zero-duration instant. Zeno’s exact wording is not preserved, so accounts of the argument are reconstructions from later reports.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is there a difference between being at rest at an instant and being in motion at that instant?
2. Could a film frame capture motion?
3. Does it matter whether time is continuous or made of indivisible moments?
4. Is “velocity at an instant” a real property or a mathematical convenience?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The Stadium is a separate argument about rows of bodies moving in opposite directions, which Aristotle also reports in *Physics* VI.9. It concerns relative motion and should not be treated as simply another version of the Arrow.
