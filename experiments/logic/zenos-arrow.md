# Zeno’s Arrow

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Zeno of Elea  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Greek, fifth century BCE; known through later reports  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** motion, instants, continuity  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Dichotomy](zenos-dichotomy.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Consider an arrow in flight. At any single instant, the arrow occupies a region of space exactly its own size. It does not move during that instant, because an instant has no duration in which to move. If time consists of instants, then at every instant the arrow is at rest, and a sum of rest is rest. So the arrow never moves.

## 2. The Central Question

If a moving object is at rest at each instant, what makes it a moving object?

## 3. Why It Matters

The paradox focuses on what motion consists in. It asks us to distinguish between an object’s position at an instant and its velocity, which seems to involve what happens over time. A mathematical framework answers by defining velocity as a limit over shrinking intervals, but this raises the question of whether the definition captures what we meant by motion.

It is also a test case for views about time: whether time is made of instants, and whether the present has any duration.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The at-at theory** says motion is just being at different places at different times; no further fact about the instant is needed. **Intrinsic-velocity views** hold that velocity is a feature of an object at an instant. **Process views** deny that time is composed of durationless instants. **Aristotelian responses** deny that time is composed of instants at all.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The argument does not show that arrows never move; it asks what makes them move. The calculus definition of instantaneous velocity is an answer, but some philosophers think it only defers the question. Zeno’s exact argument is reconstructed from later reports.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Is there a difference between being at rest at an instant and being in motion at that instant?
2. Could a film frame capture motion?
3. Does it matter whether time is continuous or made of indivisible moments?
4. Is “velocity at an instant” a real property or a mathematical convenience?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

A related argument, the Stadium, concerns relative motion. Contemporary physics adds the question of whether time is continuous at very small scales, which is unsettled.
