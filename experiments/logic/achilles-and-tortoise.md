# Achilles and the Tortoise

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Zeno of Elea  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Fifth century BCE; preserved and discussed by Aristotle  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** infinity, motion, limits  
**Related experiments:** [Sorites: When Does a Heap Begin?](sorites.md); [The Bootstrap / Causal-Loop Paradox](../time-and-causality/bootstrap.md)

## 1. The Scenario

A tortoise starts ahead of Achilles. Before overtaking it, Achilles must reach its starting point; meanwhile, the tortoise moves a little farther. Achilles must then reach that new point, by which time the tortoise has advanced again. There are infinitely many such stages, apparently leaving no opportunity to catch it.

For a simple calculation, suppose Achilles runs at 10 meters per second, the tortoise at 1 meter per second, and the head start is 9 meters. Their positions after t seconds are 10t and 9+t. Equality occurs at t=1. The staged description must be reconciled with this result.

## 2. The Central Question

Does crossing infinitely many successively smaller intervals require an infinite amount of time?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument makes a common inference visible: infinitely many parts are assumed to demand an infinite total. Mathematics distinguishes the number of terms from the size of their sum. The example also prompts a separate philosophical question about how a continuous physical process should be represented by an infinite mathematical description.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Convergent-series response:** the times for the successive stages are 0.9, 0.09, 0.009 seconds, and so on. Their sum is 1 second, not infinity. In the ordinary continuous model, Achilles catches the tortoise at the limiting point.

**Foundational inquiry:** the mathematical model answers the numerical objection but raises questions about continuity and infinity. Asking whether physical space has that structure is different from rejecting the calculation.

**Task distinction:** traversing an interval need not consist of separately initiating infinitely many deliberate tasks. Describing a motion with infinitely many subintervals does not automatically add a new physical operation at each boundary.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

There is no final finite-index catch-up stage at which the remaining gap becomes zero. That does not prevent a limit point from occurring after every such stage. The numerical answer does not prove that actual space is continuous or that any physically proposed supertask is possible. Zeno’s wider project also cannot be reduced to one careless arithmetic error.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Which assumption connects infinitely many stages to infinitely much time?
2. Why is there no last positive term in the series, even though its sum is finite?
3. Does a mathematical partition create additional physical actions?
4. What empirical evidence would bear on the continuity of physical space rather than on this algebra?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

The dichotomy paradox divides a journey into endlessly smaller segments; the arrow examines motion at an instant. These share concerns about motion but use different arguments. They should not all be declared solved merely by writing one geometric series.
