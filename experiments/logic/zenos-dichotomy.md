# Zeno’s Dichotomy

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Zeno of Elea  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Greek, fifth century BCE; known through later reports  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** infinity, motion, infinite series  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Arrow](zenos-arrow.md); [Thomson’s Lamp](../mathematics/thomsons-lamp.md)

## 1. The Scenario

To walk across a room, you must first reach the halfway point. Before that, you must reach the halfway point of that half, and so on without end. It seems that to get anywhere you must complete an infinite number of tasks, each of which takes some time. No one can finish an infinite series of tasks, so, Zeno’s argument suggests, you can never begin to move, or never arrive.

## 2. The Central Question

How can a journey be completed if it can always be divided into infinitely many smaller stages?

## 3. Why It Matters

The paradox was an early challenge to the idea that space and time can be divided without limit. The standard mathematical reply uses convergent series: infinitely many ever-shorter stages can take a finite total time. That answers the arithmetic, but a philosophical question remains about whether completing an infinite sequence of actions is possible.

The Dichotomy, together with Achilles and the Tortoise, is where discussions of infinity and the continuum often begin.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The mathematical reply** says that the sum of the stages is finite, so no contradiction follows. **Aristotle’s reply** distinguishes potential from actual infinity: the stages are potential divisions, not completed acts. **Supertask views** investigate whether infinite sequences can be completed. **Discrete views** deny that space and time are infinitely divisible. The reply chosen affects the others.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox is not refuted merely by pointing out that people walk. Zeno’s question is what must be true if motion is possible. It is also not exactly the same as Achilles and the Tortoise: this version concerns reaching a goal and not catching a moving target. Zeno’s own wording is lost and is reconstructed from later authors.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does the convergent sum fully answer the objection, or only the arithmetic part?
2. Is each of the infinitely many stages an action you perform?
3. What if space were made of indivisible minimum units?
4. How does the problem differ if you move at ever-decreasing speed?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Reversed versions argue that you could never start, since you must first complete a prior stage with no first step. Thomson’s lamp generalizes the issue to infinite sequences of discrete acts.
