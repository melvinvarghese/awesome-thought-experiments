# Zeno’s Dichotomy

**Category:** logic  
**Type:** paradox  
**Associated thinker(s):** Zeno of Elea  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Ancient Greek, fifth century BCE; known through later reports  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Themes:** infinity, motion, infinite series  
**Related experiments:** [Achilles and the Tortoise](achilles-and-tortoise.md); [Zeno’s Arrow](zenos-arrow.md); [Thomson’s Lamp](../mathematics/thomsons-lamp.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Use this as a teaching reconstruction, not a quotation from Zeno. To cross a room, you must first reach the halfway point; before that, the halfway point to it; and so on, producing ever-shorter distances. Aristotle’s later report says the Dichotomy argues that a moving body cannot reach its goal without first reaching the halfway point (*Physics* VI.9, 239b11). In the familiar reconstruction, the infinitely many required subdivisions seem to make arrival impossible. The argument leaves open whether these subdivisions are separate actions or parts of one continuous run.

## 2. The Central Question

How can a journey be completed if it can always be divided into infinitely many smaller stages?

## 3. Why It Matters

Under a constant-speed model, the times for successive halves also form a geometric series: half the total time, then a quarter, then an eighth, and so on. In standard real analysis, that series has a finite sum. This answers the arithmetic objection under that model; it does not by itself settle whether an infinite sequence of discrete acts can be completed or whether the continuum accurately describes physical space and time.

The Dichotomy and Achilles and the Tortoise share a regress structure, but the latter concerns catching a moving target rather than reaching a fixed goal.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Modern mathematical reply:** The geometric series for the successive distances or durations converges, so infinitely many terms need not have an infinite total. **Aristotle’s response:** In *Physics* III.6 he treats magnitude as potentially divisible without end rather than an actually completed infinite; in *Physics* VI.9 he argues that time, like magnitude, is infinitely divisible. Nick Huggett’s modern reconstruction distinguishes a continuous run that is potentially divisible from an actually segmented run, while noting difficulties in treating this as a complete answer. **Supertask discussions:** These ask whether infinitely many acts can be completed in finite time, a question distinct from whether the series converges. **Discrete-spacetime proposals:** These reject infinite divisibility as a physical description; that is a separate hypothesis, not a consequence of the series calculation.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The paradox is not answered merely by observing that people walk: it asks how its reasoning should be answered. It is not exactly the same as Achilles and the Tortoise, which concerns catching a moving target. Zeno’s own book is lost, and ancient witnesses transmit the arguments largely in paraphrase, so the familiar step-by-step presentation is a reconstruction rather than Zeno’s surviving wording.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Does the convergent sum fully answer the objection, or only its arithmetic part?
2. Is each of the infinitely many stages a separate action, or a way of dividing one continuous journey?
3. What would change if physical space or time were discrete?
4. How does the issue change if the runner’s speed decreases at each stage?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

A later regressive formulation traces the journey backward: if the runner arrives, there is no earliest segment in the backward sequence. It is a useful variation, not a separately established quotation from Zeno. Thomson’s lamp raises a related but distinct supertask question about infinitely many discrete switchings; it should not be identified with the Dichotomy’s continuous-run reconstruction.

## 10. Sources and Attribution

Zeno’s book is lost. The Dichotomy is known through ancient testimony, including Aristotle’s *Physics* VI.9; the familiar step-by-step version here follows modern scholarly reconstruction. Aristotle’s account of potential infinity and his response to the Dichotomy are distinct from the modern convergent-series explanation. The latter establishes a finite sum within the stated mathematical model, not by itself the physical or metaphysical possibility of every supertask. The walking and lamp comparisons are explanatory framing, not wording attributed to Zeno. Interpretations of Aristotle’s reply and the possibility of completing supertasks remain open to discussion.
