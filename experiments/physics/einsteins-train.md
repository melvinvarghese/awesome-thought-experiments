# Einstein’s Train and the Relativity of Simultaneity

**Category:** physics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Albert Einstein  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Einstein, Relativity: The Special and General Theory (1916; English translation 1920)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** simultaneity, special relativity, reference frames  
**Related experiments:** [The Twin Paradox](twin-paradox.md); [Einstein Chases a Light Beam](einsteins-light-beam.md); [McTaggart’s Paradox of Time](../time-and-causality/mctaggarts-paradox.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Two lightning bolts strike the ends of a long railway embankment, at points A and B. Einstein asks what it means to call these distant events simultaneous. In the embankment frame, an observer at the midpoint receives both flashes together; with the equal-distance and standard light-synchronization assumptions, this is the operational criterion for assigning the strikes the same time in that frame. A simultaneous-looking arrival is not, by itself, a frame-independent verdict about distant events.

A train moves along the track toward B. At the instant the strikes occur, an observer at the train's midpoint is level with the embankment observer and is moving toward the light from B and away from the light from A. The train observer therefore receives the B flash first. To infer when the distant strikes occurred, however, arrival times must be interpreted using clocks synchronized in the relevant frame; under the standard light-synchronization convention in the train frame, the B strike is assigned an earlier time.

## 2. The Central Question

If each inertial frame assigns the same speed c to light in vacuum, will the observers assign the same time to these distant strikes?

## 3. Why It Matters

Under Einstein's standard synchronization of clocks, they will not. The embankment frame assigns the strikes the same time, while the train frame assigns the B strike an earlier time. These are frame-relative coordinate assignments for the same events; the difference is not merely a discrepancy in what the observers happen to see.

This conflicts with a Newtonian universal “now.” Relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, and length contraction are related consequences of the special-relativistic postulates and Lorentz transformations; the twin paradox is a further comparison of elapsed proper time along different worldlines, not an effect caused by simultaneity alone.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The standard view** treats distant simultaneity as relative to an inertial frame. **Conventionalists** argue that synchronizing distant clocks involves a convention; the scope and force of this thesis remain debated. **Lorentzian interpretations** can posit a preferred frame while preserving empirical predictions indistinguishable from those of special relativity. **Philosophers of time** disagree about what relativity of simultaneity implies for an objective present; it challenges a unique frame-independent present but does not, by itself, settle every metaphysical account of time.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The relativity of simultaneity does not mean that the order of causally connected events can be reversed: if a light-speed-or-slower signal could connect two events, all inertial frames preserve their causal order. The two lightning strikes in the example are spacelike separated, so their temporal order can differ between frames. Nor is the result merely that observers receive light at different times because light takes time to travel. Einstein distinguishes arrival from the frame-based time assigned to the emission events using synchronized clocks and the standard light-signal convention. The example is a thought experiment, not a report of an experiment performed.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Why is it important that both observers use the same speed for light?
2. Which observer is right about whether the flashes were simultaneous?
3. Could two events that are causally connected change order?
4. What would be lost if there were a single universal present?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Einstein’s book offers further examples. The pole-in-the-barn and twin cases apply the same principles to length and time and should be examined separately.

## 9. Further Reading

- [Albert Einstein, Relativity: The Special and General Theory (Project Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5001) — A public-domain English translation of Einstein’s popular book, in which the train example appears.
- [Albert Einstein, “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies” (1905 English translation)](https://www.fourmilab.ch/etexts/einstein/specrel/www/) — Sections 1–2 define distant simultaneity through synchronized clocks and discuss its relativity between inertial systems.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Conventionality of Simultaneity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-convensimul/) — Philosophical discussion of simultaneity in special relativity.
- [Robert DiSalle, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Space and Time: Inertial Frames](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-iframes/) — Includes discussion of Lorentzian and Einsteinian interpretations and their empirical equivalence.
- [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 3, §5.5: The Lorentz Transformation](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/5-5-the-lorentz-transformation) — Textbook treatment of the train example, Lorentz transformations, and the causal structure of spacetime.
- [Steven Savitt, Natalja Deng, and Oliver Pooley, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Being and Becoming in Modern Physics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-bebecome/) — Surveys philosophical responses to relativity of simultaneity and the objective-present question.
