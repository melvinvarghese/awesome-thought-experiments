# Einstein’s Train and the Relativity of Simultaneity

**Category:** physics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Albert Einstein  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Einstein, Relativity: The Special and General Theory (1916; English translation 1920)  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** simultaneity, special relativity, reference frames  
**Related experiments:** [The Twin Paradox](twin-paradox.md); [Einstein Chases a Light Beam](einsteins-light-beam.md); [McTaggart’s Paradox of Time](../time-and-causality/mctaggarts-paradox.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Two lightning bolts strike the ends of a long railway embankment, at points A and B. An observer standing on the embankment, midway between A and B, sees the flashes at the same moment and concludes that the strikes were simultaneous.

A train moves along the track toward B. An observer sitting at the middle of the train, who was level with the embankment observer when the strikes occurred, is moving toward the light from B and away from the light from A. He therefore meets the flash from B first and sees it before the flash from A.

## 2. The Central Question

If both observers measure light as travelling at the same speed, can they agree on which events are simultaneous?

## 3. Why It Matters

Einstein’s answer is that they cannot. The train observer will say that B struck first; the embankment observer will say that the strikes were simultaneous, and each is correct relative to their own frame. Simultaneity is not absolute.

This overturns the Newtonian assumption of a universal “now” and leads to the other effects of special relativity, such as time dilation and the twin paradox.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**The standard view** treats simultaneity as relative to a frame. **Conventionalists** hold that the synchronization of distant clocks involves a convention. **Neo-Lorentzian views** retain a preferred frame that cannot be detected. **Philosophers of time** debate whether relativity of simultaneity undermines the idea of an objective present.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The relativity of simultaneity does not mean that the order of causally connected events can be reversed. It also does not mean that observers merely see things at different times because light takes time to travel; Einstein’s analysis corrects for that. The example is a pedagogical reconstruction, not a record of an experiment.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. Why is it important that both observers use the same speed for light?
2. Which observer is right about whether the flashes were simultaneous?
3. Could two events that are causally connected change order?
4. What would be lost if there were a single universal present?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Einstein’s book offers further examples. The pole-in-the-barn and twin cases apply the same principles to length and time and should be examined separately.
