# Newton’s Bucket

**Category:** physics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Isaac Newton  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Newton, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), Scholium on space, time and motion
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** absolute space, rotation, inertia  
**Related experiments:** [Einstein’s Elevator](einsteins-elevator.md); [The Hole Argument](hole-argument.md); [Galileo’s Ship](galileos-ship.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Newton’s stated sequence begins with a bucket suspended from a long cord, the cord twisted, and the bucket and water held at rest together. He then sets the vessel turning in the opposite direction as the cord unwinds. At first the water’s surface remains flat while the vessel moves relative to the water. As the vessel gradually communicates motion to the water, the water rotates, recedes from the middle, and rises at the sides into a concave surface. When water and vessel rotate in the same time, they are at relative rest and the water’s rise is greatest. Newton says he had experienced the concave surface. His passage does not include a later stage in which the bucket is stopped; that familiar continuation is a variation, not part of the sequence he describes.

Across Newton’s two endpoint stages, the surface’s curvature does not track the water’s rotation relative to the bucket: that relative motion is greatest while the surface is flat, and disappears when the concavity is greatest.

## 2. The Central Question

If the water’s concavity does not depend on motion relative to the bucket, what does it depend on?

## 3. Why It Matters

Newton treats the water’s rise as an indicator of its centrifugal “endeavour” to recede from the axis, and that effect as a measure of true circular motion. He contrasts the initial stage, when relative motion between water and bucket is greatest but the surface is flat, with the final stage, when they are at relative rest but the surface’s rise is greatest. He infers that this effect does not depend on the water’s translation relative to surrounding bodies and that true circular motion cannot be defined by such translations. In Newton’s framework, true motion is motion through absolute space. This is his argument against identifying mechanically significant rotation with motion relative to nearby bodies; the experiment does not by itself show that space is a substance or settle whether his inference succeeds.

The argument became part of a long dispute over how to understand true motion and inertial effects. Later responses differ: Leibniz rejected Newtonian absolute space but did not simply identify true motion with motion relative to distant matter; Mach emphasized the relation of inertia to the wider distribution of matter. Neither response should be treated as a single settled relationist alternative.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Newton’s view** identifies true circular motion with motion through absolute space and treats centrifugal effects as evidence that relative rotation against the bucket is not the relevant standard. **Later relationist views** are not interchangeable: Leibniz offered a different account of true motion, while Mach argued that inertial effects should be understood in relation to the wider masses of the universe. Mach specifically discussed rotation relative to Earth and the celestial bodies and imagined that sufficiently massive bucket walls might affect the water; this is a proposal, not a complete alternative mechanics. **General relativity** replaces fixed Newtonian space with dynamical spacetime geometry whose metric encodes local inertial structure and is partly shaped by matter and energy. But general relativity does not, in all its solutions, make inertial structure fully dependent on matter distribution, even though some models exhibit Machian effects such as frame-dragging. The debate therefore concerns differing theories and interpretations, not a simple progression from Newton to one agreed answer.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The bucket does not prove that space is a substance. Newton argues that rotation relative to the bucket alone does not account for the observed centrifugal effect; he does not claim that the bucket is the only possible relative-motion standard. He wrote that the water’s concavity was “as I have experienced,” which records his report, not independent confirmation of the experiment’s historical performance. His argument concerns rotation, not absolute position or uniform speed.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What would happen in an otherwise empty universe if a bucket of water rotated?
2. Can a relationist account explain the concavity of the water without absolute space?
3. Is spacetime structure the same as Newton’s absolute space?
4. Does Galileo’s ship, where uniform motion cannot be detected, conflict with the bucket?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Newton’s separate example of two globes connected by a cord uses tension to discuss how true rotation might be inferred when no nearby bodies are available for comparison. Mach questioned what could be concluded about such an otherwise empty universe and proposed that the wider distribution of matter matters to inertia. These arguments differ over what explains inertial effects; they are not the same thought experiment.
