# Newton’s Bucket

**Category:** physics  
**Type:** thought experiment  
**Associated thinker(s):** Isaac Newton  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Newton, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), Scholium on space, time and motion  
**Difficulty:** Intermediate  
**Themes:** absolute space, rotation, inertia  
**Related experiments:** [Einstein’s Elevator](einsteins-elevator.md); [The Hole Argument](hole-argument.md); [Galileo’s Ship](galileos-ship.md)

## 1. The Scenario

Hang a bucket of water from a long cord and twist the cord tightly. Release it, and the bucket begins to spin. At first, the water stays still and its surface is flat, even though the bucket is moving relative to it. Gradually, the water begins to rotate with the bucket, and its surface becomes concave as it climbs the sides. If the bucket is then stopped, the water continues to spin for a while, with a concave surface, though the bucket is at rest relative to it.

The shape of the surface seems not to depend on how the water moves relative to the bucket.

## 2. The Central Question

If the water’s concavity does not depend on motion relative to the bucket, what does it depend on?

## 3. Why It Matters

Newton used the bucket to argue for absolute space: rotation relative to space itself, and not merely relative to nearby bodies, produces inertial effects. If motion were purely relative, the water’s shape would track its relation to the bucket. It does not, so there must be more to motion than relative motion.

The argument opened a long dispute over whether space is a substance or a system of relations, which continued with Leibniz, Berkeley, Mach, and general relativity.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Newtonian views** accept absolute space as the standard of rotation. **Relationalists** reply that the water’s motion relative to distant matter might explain its shape. **Mach’s idea** is that inertia is due to the distribution of mass in the universe. **Relativistic views** replace absolute space with spacetime structure, which some regard as a form of absolute structure. The debate continues.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The bucket does not prove that space is a substance; it shows that the relative motion of water and bucket is not what matters. Newton himself reported actual experiments with a rotating bucket. Newton’s argument concerns absolute rotation, not absolute position or speed.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What would happen in an otherwise empty universe if a bucket of water rotated?
2. Can a relationist account explain the concavity of the water without absolute space?
3. Is spacetime structure the same as Newton’s absolute space?
4. Does Galileo’s ship, where uniform motion cannot be detected, conflict with the bucket?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

Newton’s second argument, with two globes connected by a cord, makes a similar point. Mach’s reply imagines the stars removed. These variations differ in what they take to be the source of inertial effects.
