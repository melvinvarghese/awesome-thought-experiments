# The Hole Argument

**Category:** physics  
**Type:** argument  
**Associated thinker(s):** Albert Einstein, John Earman, John Norton  
**Original publication or approximate period:** Einstein’s 1913–15 hole argument; revived by Earman and Norton (1987)  
**Difficulty:** Advanced  
**Themes:** spacetime, determinism, general covariance  
**Related experiments:** [Newton’s Bucket](newtons-bucket.md); [Laplace’s Demon](../time-and-causality/laplaces-demon.md); [Einstein’s Elevator](einsteins-elevator.md)

## 1. The Scenario

In general relativity, the geometry of spacetime is described by a metric field that satisfies Einstein’s equations. These equations are generally covariant: a smooth re-mapping of the points of spacetime takes one solution into another.

Now take a region, the hole, in which there is no matter. Choose a transformation that is the identity outside the hole and smoothly changes things inside. It gives a new solution that agrees with the old one everywhere outside the hole and differs inside. If the points of spacetime are real things that carry the metric, then two different solutions agree up to the hole and diverge after it, so the theory fails to determine what happens in the hole from what happens outside.

## 2. The Central Question

Does general relativity force us to say either that spacetime points lack an identity apart from the fields on them, or that the theory is indeterministic?

## 3. Why It Matters

The argument shows that a theory’s mathematical symmetries can have metaphysical consequences. Einstein first used it to reject generally covariant equations, before changing his mind. Later philosophers used it to challenge the view that spacetime is a substance.

It also bears on the question of what counts as a physical possibility: whether the two solutions are distinct possibilities or merely descriptions of one.

## 4. Major Interpretations

**Relationists** say the argument shows that points have no identity apart from relations. **Sophisticated substantivalists** say that points have their identity but that diffeomorphic models represent the same situation. **Structuralists** say that only structures matter. **Those who accept indeterminism** say that the theory is indeterministic in a harmless way. Each response concerns what it is to represent a world.

## 5. Common Misunderstandings

The hole is a region of spacetime, not a physical object, and the argument does not describe an actual place with missing matter. It does not show that general relativity is wrong. Its modern significance depends on assumptions about how mathematical models represent physical situations.

## 6. Think for Yourself

1. What would it mean for two solutions that differ only by a re-mapping to be the same world?
2. Does the answer affect whether the future is open?
3. How does the argument relate to Newton’s bucket?
4. What do you think counts as a physical difference?

## 7. Variations and Extensions

There are variants for other field theories and for gauge symmetries. Discussions of them tend to separate the technical issues from the metaphysical ones.
