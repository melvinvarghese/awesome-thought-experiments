# Editorial guidelines

## Scope and intellectual honesty

An entry must have a recognizable scenario, a substantive question, and credible scholarly support. Label formal paradoxes, empirical paradigms, mathematical puzzles, and game models by their actual type. Do not describe a probability calculation as an unresolved philosophical mystery when its assumptions determine an answer. Conversely, do not present a contested philosophical position as a proven result.

## Attribution

Distinguish original formulations, precursors, later variants, and popular labels. Give publication titles and years only when the inspected sources support them. A modern encyclopedia is evidence for its account; it is not direct inspection of the original book. Record that distinction in Sources and Attribution. Use null in the dataset for unknown historical metadata. Do not turn a plausible attribution into an assertion of priority.

## Sources

Each entry needs an academic source actually inspected by a contributor; two independent sources and an original publication are preferred. Use university encyclopedias, scholarly books, journal articles, or established scientific institutions. Reference records include a support statement so readers know their scope. Sources shared by multiple entries should be relevant to each. Do not imply that retrieval verifies every sentence or settles scholarly disagreement.

## Writing and interpretation

Start with the scenario in ordinary language and state its assumptions. Separate the question from proposed answers. Explain unfamiliar terms. Give at least two substantial responses where appropriate; a mathematical derivation may instead contrast valid reasoning with a diagnosed misconception. Avoid false balance with empirically refuted claims. Use “under these assumptions” when changing a premise changes the result.

Article sections are Scenario, Central Question, Why It Matters, Major Interpretations, Common Misunderstandings, Think for Yourself, Variations and Extensions, Related Thought Experiments, Further Reading, and Sources and Attribution. Number these 1–10. Preserve cross-references when renaming files.

## Difficulty

- **Beginner:** no specialist prerequisites; one main conceptual distinction.
- **Intermediate:** several competing arguments or some probability, logic, or philosophical vocabulary.
- **Advanced:** technical formal assumptions or a substantial theoretical background.

Difficulty measures prerequisites, not importance. An easy-to-state scenario may have advanced interpretations.

## Relationships and review

Link entries because they share a precise problem, challenge each other's premises, or use a contrasting method. Explain the relationship in prose. Avoid reciprocal links added only to inflate connectivity. Review the scenario, attribution, source coverage, reasoning, and accessibility separately. Check the dataset and README after every addition. Run structural validation, then read the article as a new learner: automated checks cannot judge philosophical quality.

## Copyright and respectful treatment

Use original paraphrases, attribute ideas, and quote sparingly. External scholarship keeps its own rights. Sensitive scenarios may involve death, bodily autonomy, discrimination, or coercion; describe these clearly without sensational detail or assuming readers share one moral framework.
